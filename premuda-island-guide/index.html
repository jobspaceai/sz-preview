Premuda: the Cathedral Under the Sea and a Battleship on Film

In this article

Premuda is the outermost inhabited island of the Zadar archipelago: nine kilometres of stone facing the open Adriatic, 88 metres high at its highest point, with about sixty people on it. It is also, by some distance, responsible for the most famous piece of seabed in Croatia and for a date that the Italian navy still marks every year.

An island that has been emptied before

Premuda first appears in the Tabula Peutingeriana, the late Roman road map, under the name Pamodos. Its history since then is a cycle of being emptied and refilled. Ottoman corsairs raided it through the 16th and 17th centuries hard enough that by 1608 there were 26 people left on the island. Immigration rebuilt it, and by 1760 the population was 322. It has been falling since. Electricity arrived in 1971, the telephone network in 1998, and the school, open since 1867, closed in 1991 for want of pupils.

That history is written into the place. There is one village, one harbour, no traffic, and a permanent population that fits into two rows of a church.

The Cathedral

In Široka bay on the western coast, the side that takes the open sea, the limestone has been worked by wave action into a system of connected caves and tunnels that divers call the Katedrala, the Cathedral. The name is descriptive rather than romantic. The chambers are tall and vaulted, the upper entrance sits at about 11 metres and the lower at about 30, the floor is pale sand, and light comes down through openings in the roof in solid columns.

At midday, when the sun is high enough to drop straight through, the light hits the sand, bounces, and fills the chambers with the sort of blue that people describe badly for the rest of their lives. Divers compare it to standing under a rose window. The walls carry sponges, corals and polychaetes, and there are moray eels in the cracks, so keep your hands where they belong.

There are no currents in the cave system and it can be dived year round, though May to September is when the light and the water temperature cooperate. It requires a certified guide and proper equipment. This is not a snorkel stop.

10 June 1918

Eight nautical miles off Premuda lies the wreck that gives the island its second reputation. Before dawn on 10 June 1918, two small Italian motor torpedo boats, MAS 15 and MAS 21, under Luigi Rizzo, slipped in among an Austro-Hungarian squadron heading south toward the Otranto barrage. MAS 15 put two torpedoes into the dreadnought SMS Szent István. She flooded, lost power, and at 06:05 rolled over and sank.

What makes the sinking unusual is that somebody was filming. A cameraman on an accompanying ship recorded the capsize, and the footage of a 20,000-tonne battleship rolling onto her side with men walking down the hull is the only film of a battleship sinking made during the First World War. It has been in documentaries ever since, usually without anyone mentioning where it happened.

She lies upside down at 68 metres, was located again in the 1970s, and is a protected site under the Croatian Ministry of Culture. Diving her requires technical certification and a permit, and it is not something you decide on the morning. The background is in our article on the Szent István wreck, and the rest of the archipelago's wrecks are in the shipwreck guide.

Why Italy still marks the date

The Italian navy celebrates its Navy Day on 10 June, and it does so because of what happened here. Two motorboats sinking a dreadnought was the sort of result that gets turned into doctrine, and Rizzo became a national figure on the strength of it. The action is remembered in Italy as the Battle of Premuda, which is a generous name for an engagement that lasted a few minutes, and in Croatia mostly as the place where a very large ship went down.

Above the water

The island itself is white pebble beaches, low stone walls, one harbour and a great deal of silence. The water is exceptionally clear for the straightforward reason that there is nothing on the island to make it otherwise: no industry, no river, almost no people. In September the place is close to empty, and the swimming is as good as anywhere in this sea.

Practicalities are thin. There is a ferry a few times a week, a couple of places to eat that open in season, and rooms rather than hotels. Water and power arrived late and are still treated with respect by people who remember them arriving. If you are coming for a day on your own keel, plan to be self-sufficient, and check the forecast twice: the western side of Premuda has nothing between it and Italy, and a bura that is merely inconvenient in the Zadar channel is a different animal out here.

Getting there

Premuda is a long way out. This is a speedboat destination or a leg of a multi-day charter, not a casual afternoon, and the western approach is exposed enough that the forecast decides the plan rather than the other way round. Read our guide to the bura, jugo and maestral before you commit to a date out here.

Diving the wreck needs a licensed operator and deep-diving certification. The Cathedral needs a guide. But you can anchor above both, swim in water that has nothing upstream of it, and know exactly what is underneath you.
