Lučica Bay: Two Shipwrecks in Four Metres of Water

There is a bay on Dugi Otok, near the village of Soline, where the archipelago's war is still visible from the surface. Lučica is a small, sheltered anchorage, and it holds two wrecks. Neither of them is deep. One of them is in four metres of water.

The bomber's work

In the western part of the bay lies a cargo ship from the Second World War, hit by an Italian bomber. She rests in two to four metres of water.

That figure is worth pausing on. Not twenty metres. Not forty. Four. On a calm day the hull is a shadow you can pick out from the deck of your own boat, and a mask plus a couple of metres of free diving is enough to swim the length of her and look into what is left.

The wider context is not obscure. The Adriatic between 1941 and 1945 was a contested supply route, and both sides went after shipping from the air. The seabed off the island of Pag alone holds a documented collection of warship wrecks from both world wars. What is unusual about the Lučica ship is not that she was sunk from the air but that she was sunk in a bay so shallow that eighty years later she is still an object rather than a debris field.

The second wreck

On the eastern side of the same bay, in the shallows, lies a Croatian passenger vessel. Two wrecks, one anchorage, both within snorkelling reach of the same swim.

Nobody has made a heritage site out of either. There is no ticket, no pontoon, no interpretation panel, no guide with a laminated card. The bay is simply a bay that happens to have two ships in it, and the only reason anybody knows is that the water is clear and the depth is trivial.

Why four metres changes the experience

Most wreck sites in Croatia are for divers. That means a course, a cylinder, a buddy, a briefing, an operator, a certification card and, realistically, a couple of hundred euros and a day. It also means the people who most want to see a shipwreck, which in our experience is children between eight and fourteen, are the exact people who cannot.

Lučica removes all of that. Anyone who can swim can see this. Grandparents who prefer a mask to a tank can see it. A ten-year-old with a snorkel and no fear can spend an hour over it and come out of the water genuinely changed, because a real ship on the bottom is a completely different category of object from anything in a museum.

Shallow also means light. At four metres in Adriatic water on a sunny day you are looking at the wreck in full colour, not the blue-grey monochrome you get below twenty. Photographs from here look like photographs of a ship, not of a shadow.

Where this sits on the island

Soline lies in the deeply indented Solišćica bay on the eastern, sheltered side of Dugi Otok. The name comes from salt: soline is Croatian for salt pans. It is a small place with a long shoreline and very little happening, which describes most of this coast north of Sali.

This is the quiet half of an already quiet island. The famous parts of Dugi Otok are elsewhere: Sakarun in the north, Telašćica and Lake Mir in the south, the caves on the western cliffs. The eastern shore between them is where the island actually lives, and it gets almost no visitors after September.

Getting there, honestly

Lučica is not on our standard tour routes today. It sits well up the coast of Dugi Otok, and reaching it properly means a long day rather than a detour. We would rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise.

What we can tell you is that the bay has a mooring buoy, so tying up is straightforward, and that it is an easy addition to a private charter with the hours to get there. We are also adding a motor boat to the fleet for next season specifically to reach places at this end of the archipelago, and Lučica is on the shortlist.

In the meantime, if you are cruising this coast on your own keel, put it on the chart. And if you want the wreck experience without the long run north, the Ledenik off Sestrunj is half an hour from Zadar and does much the same job for a snorkeller. The full list of wrecks in this archipelago runs from two metres to sixty six, and we can usually fit one of them into a day.
