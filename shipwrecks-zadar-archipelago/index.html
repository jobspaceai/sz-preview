The Shipwrecks of the Zadar Archipelago: Four Ships, Four Stories

Every stretch of busy sea keeps a record of its accidents, and the Zadar archipelago is no different. What makes this particular seabed unusual is the range. Within a couple of hours of sailing you can pass over a First World War battleship lying upside down at 66 metres, a cargo ship that sank in living memory, a freighter whose bow stood above the waterline for decades, and a bay where two wrecks sit in water shallow enough to stand in.

Four wrecks, four completely different stories, and four completely different ways of seeing them. Here is what is down there and what it takes to get a look at each one.

Why this seabed is so crowded

The geography explains most of it. The Zadar archipelago is a set of long, parallel limestone ridges separated by narrow channels, and those channels have been the sheltered inside route up and down the eastern Adriatic for as long as anyone has been sailing here. Shelter attracts traffic. Traffic plus rock plus bura plus, in two world wars, aircraft, produces wrecks.

The Croatian Adriatic holds several hundred documented wrecks. Most of them are anonymous, broken, or too deep to matter to anyone without technical training. The four below are the ones worth knowing about if you are sailing out of Zadar, because between them they cover every depth from two metres to sixty six.

Szent István, 1918: the deep one

The Austro-Hungarian battleship Szent István was hit by two torpedoes from the Italian motor boat MAS 15 shortly after three in the morning on 10 June 1918, off the island of Premuda. She stayed afloat for nearly three hours, then rolled over and went down at 6:12, taking 89 men with her. A cameraman on the sister ship Tegetthoff filmed the whole thing, which makes her the only battleship of the First World War whose sinking exists on film.

She lies upside down at 66 metres. The wreck was located in the mid-1970s and is protected by the Croatian Ministry of Culture, which in practice means technical divers with permits and nobody else. For everyone else, it is a place to anchor above, look at sixty six metres of blue water, and think about what is directly underneath the keel.

Ledenik, 2008: the one you can snorkel

A refrigerated cargo ship built in 1962 and registered in Zadar, roughly seventy metres long, the Ledenik sank on 23 October 2008 about two hundred metres off the northern shore of Sestrunj. She lies on her side on a sandy bottom, with the deepest part under thirty metres and her shallowest structure around ten. Divers usually work her at about twenty.

That ten metre figure is what makes her special. On a calm day with good light the outline of the hull is visible from the surface, which means a reasonably fit snorkeller can look down on an entire, intact, seventy metre ship without a tank, a course or a briefing. She is the most accessible real wreck in the archipelago and the newest, which is why she is still in one piece.

Michele, 1983: the freighter with a rumour attached

On 11 June 1983 the Italian cargo ship Michele, 76 metres long, 10 metres wide and rated at 500 tonnes, ran aground on the shallows near the islet of Lagnići, off the northern cape of Dugi Otok not far from the Veli Rat lighthouse. She was carrying artificial fertiliser.

The circumstances have never been entirely settled. The version the island tells, recorded by lighthouse keeper Zvonimir Škvorčević in the book Legende svjetla, is that the crew put her on the shallows deliberately during bad weather in order to collect on the insurance. Nobody has proved it and nobody has disproved it either.

For years she sat with her bow and forward mast clear of the water, visible from a long way off, and Gibonni filmed a music video on her. Four decades of weather have taken the superstructure completely. What is left is the hull, resting at around six metres, with the stern the best preserved part and sections of deck at snorkelling depth. It is one of the least demanding wreck dives on this coast and a genuinely easy snorkel.

Lučica: two wrecks, four metres of water

And then there is Lučica bay near Soline on Dugi Otok. In the western part of the bay lies a Second World War cargo ship hit by an Italian bomber, sitting in two to four metres of water. On the eastern side, in the shallows, there is a Croatian passenger vessel.

Two wrecks, one anchorage, no diving gear required for either. It is the only place in the archipelago where you can show a child a wartime shipwreck and hand them nothing more complicated than a mask.

What you can actually see, and how

Sorted by what you need to bring:

Mask and nothing else: Lučica, both wrecks. Michele, the shallow parts.

Mask plus decent breath-holding: Ledenik, from above. Michele, properly.

Recreational diving certificate: Ledenik at twenty metres, which is a sunlit, no-decompression dive, and the Katedrala cave system at Premuda, a connected set of chambers between ten and thirty metres that is bright enough for beginners with a guide.

Technical certification and a permit from the Ministry of Culture: Szent István. That is the whole list.

Getting to them from Zadar

None of these sites are on a bus route, which is exactly why they are still worth seeing. Ledenik is about half an hour out of the city at speedboat pace and pairs naturally with the usual swim stops. Michele sits at the top end of Dugi Otok, which puts it in reach of a long day combined with Sakarun and the lighthouse. Premuda and the water above Szent István are a full day on the outer archipelago. Lučica needs a private charter with the time to get up the coast.

A speedboat day covers the near ones comfortably. A multi-day sail covers the far ones without rushing. Either way, ask about it when you book: wreck stops depend on sea state and light, and a skipper who knows the ground will tell you honestly whether today is the day.
