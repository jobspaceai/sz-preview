The Church of the Holy Cross in Nin: a Building That Tells the Time

Seventeen kilometres north of Zadar, in the old town of Nin, stands a whitewashed church you can walk around in twenty paces. Depending on which specialist you ask it went up in the 9th century or the 11th. What brings people out is not the age but a claim about the walls: that they are crooked on purpose, and that the crookedness follows the sun.

The smallest cathedral that never was one

The Church of the Holy Cross is 7.80 metres long, 7.60 wide and 8.20 high inside, with walls 57 centimetres thick. The plan is a Greek cross with four rectangular arms of near enough equal length, the eastern one closed by a rounded apse. Corner squinches over the crossing carry a cylindrical drum with a dome hidden inside it, broken up outside by tall shallow niches. It stands on the remains of Roman houses, their foundations still visible in the grass, along with some 170 medieval graves.

Thomas Graham Jackson, the English architect who toured this coast for three volumes published in 1887, is credited with calling it the smallest cathedral in the world. It was never a cathedral. Nin was a bishopric from the 9th century to the 19th, and the seat of it was St Anselm's, a few streets away. Holy Cross belonged to the local counts: apostolic visitors record it in 1579 under their patronage and in 1603 as town property, and a note of 1697 calls it the count's oratory.

You will also read that it is the oldest fully preserved church in the world. Its own tourist board makes a narrower and far more defensible claim: the only sacred building in Nin untouched since it was built, and the most valuable surviving monument of early Croatian church architecture. A world record it is not. It stands open during the day, free to walk into and essentially empty, and no source we could find describes regular services being held in it.

The irregularities are the point

Measure the building and nothing comes out square. The crossing is a trapezoid, the axes refuse to line up, the drum is not a true circle. This was long read as the work of masons who could not do better.

The art historian Mladen Pejaković argued the opposite. In "Broj iz svjetlosti" (Number from Light), published in Zagreb in 1978 and in Italian as "Le pietre e il sole" in 1982, he made the case that the deviations are the plan rather than a failure of it: the openings were placed so light would land on defined points on defined days, making the building a calendar and a sundial. He backed it with survey drawings, calculations and photographs by Nenad Gattin.

Nin has built an annual event on it. The Festival of the Sun and Light was first held on the first day of summer in 2009, the International Year of Astronomy, and still runs. On the solstice the light can be followed in the central apse for about two hours, roughly 10:20 to 12:30. A curator at the Nin museum notes the paradox: on the longest day of the year, the projection lasts the shortest.

What the conservators found in 1969

The church was surveyed and repaired in 1969 by the Zadar conservation institute under Ksenija Radulić, overseen by a commission that included Ivo Petricioli and Milan Prelog. Pavuša Vežić worked on it as a trainee and later published the results.

Two of them matter here, and they pull in opposite directions. In favour of the sundial reading: two narrow windows turned up in the small side apses flanking the eastern arm, openings nobody had known about. Against it: Vežić records that the square windows in the west walls of the side arms are probably not original, and that the window in the drum is an insert into an older, wider one. If some openings are later, a reading built on today's window positions stands on ground that moves.

So, plainly. Pejaković's thesis is one researcher's interpretation, popularised and enthusiastically adopted by the tourist board, which likes to invoke Stonehenge. The mainstream literature dates and classifies the building without leaning on it. Both hold: the light effects are real and you can watch them, and their intended purpose is not settled.

Godečaj's lintel and the argument about the date

The lintel over the door is a carved block about 142 by 40 centimetres, worked in two bands: ten interlaced knotted circles along the top, a vine scroll below. On its underside runs an inscription naming a župan called Godečaj who raised, or fitted out, this house.

That inscription carries the whole dating argument. Older scholarship placed it between 788 and 800, which would make it among the earliest Croatian inscriptions anywhere. Ljubo Karaman accepted that in 1930 and moved away from it a year later. Vežić argues for the 11th century on three grounds: the letter forms, particularly the shape used in Godečaj's name, which palaeographers say does not appear here earlier; the density of the interlace, which matches 11th-century carving elsewhere in Dalmatia; and the niched drum, which has close 11th-century parallels from Anatolia to Emilia Romagna.

The sign at the door still says IX. st., and plenty of specialists hold to it. One thing makes the building matter either way. It had a near twin in Zadar, the chapel of St Vitus, demolished long ago and surviving only in a drawing from 1877. Nin is where you go to see what Zadar lost.

Nin was the capital while Zadar was still Byzantine

The old core of Nin sits on a small island in a shallow lagoon, joined to the mainland by two stone bridges. The Liburnians founded it in the 9th century BC and called it Aenona. Rome made it a municipium with an aqueduct, a forum, one of the largest temples on this coast and a port at what is now Zaton.

Then it became the first political and religious centre of medieval Croatia and the oldest Croatian royal town, seat of the dukes Višeslav, Trpimir and Branimir and of the kings Tomislav, Petar Krešimir IV and Zvonimir. Its bishops mattered out of all proportion to the size of the place, Gregory of Nin above all, who fought to keep Glagolitic in the liturgy. All of it while Zadar, twenty minutes down the road, was a Byzantine city facing the other way. It ended in 1646, when Venice destroyed Nin deliberately so the Ottomans could not use it against Zadar.

Getting there, and why you cannot sail in

Nin is 17 kilometres north of Zadar with buses roughly hourly, so this is a half-day trip by road, not by water. That is a depth problem, not a scheduling one. The Croatian pilot book allows vessels drawing up to one metre into Nin harbour and no more, the approach threads between a sandbank and the shore through a buoyed channel, and there are shoals and reefs off the entrance.

The locals settled this long ago with the ninska plazulja, a flat-bottomed wooden boat 5.5 to 6 metres long, built to slide over sand and be pulled up a beach. There were about thirty in the harbour before the Second World War and more than a hundred by the end of the century. Roughly fifty are left, and nothing with a keel has ever belonged here.

So split the day. Drive or take the bus up in the morning, then get on the water in the afternoon. Our day sailing trip works as the other half of the same day, the evening sail puts the old town skyline in front of you at the right hour, and the guide to the best time to sail out of Zadar covers the wind month by month.

If the sun-and-stone theme is what hooked you, there is a second stop for it offshore. The 15th meridian, the line that defines Central European Time, crosses Sestrunj about twenty miles from Nin, and the Sea Organ in Zadar belongs to the same local habit of turning buildings into instruments.
