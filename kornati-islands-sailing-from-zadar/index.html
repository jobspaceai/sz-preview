Kornati Islands from Zadar: What a Sailing Trip Really Looks Like

In 1929, a Nobel-winning playwright stood on a boat somewhere south of Zadar and ran out of clever things to say. George Bernard Shaw, a man rarely lost for words, wrote of what he saw:

"On the last day of the Creation, God desired to crown his work, and thus created the Kornati islands out of tears, stars and breath."George Bernard Shaw, 1929

Skippers quote it to this day, mostly because nobody has managed to put it better since.

What the Kornati islands actually are

The Kornati archipelago counts 89 islands, islets and reefs scattered southwest of Zadar, a national park since 1980. Most of it is bare limestone, streaked silver and gold, dropping into some of the clearest water in the Adriatic. There is no fresh water, no shade and no shop, and that is precisely the point: the Kornati are the Adriatic as it looked before anyone built anything on it.

Look closer at that bare stone, though, and you will see lines running across the islands, from one shore all the way to the other. Those are dry stone walls, about 330 kilometres of them, every metre laid by hand without a drop of mortar. Sheep have grazed here for close to five thousand years, and the walls kept each family's flock on its own ground. The islands are long and narrow, so the walls simply run coast to coast.

The shepherds who bought an archipelago

Here is the part most day-trippers never hear. In the 1630s, families from Murter and Betina, two towns on an overcrowded little island near Šibenik, began rowing out to the Kornati to tend sheep for the Zadar nobility who owned the pastures. They kept at it for two centuries. Then, in the second half of the 19th century, the shepherds did something remarkable: they bought the islands from their masters. To this day the Kornati are largely owned by Murter families, and the little stone cottages and konobas in the bays belong to their descendants. When you eat grilled fish at a konoba reachable only by boat, your host's family has probably worked that bay for ten generations.

What the sailing is like

From Zadar you enter the park after two to three hours under sail, most of it in the lee of Dugi Otok, which keeps the sea comfortable even when the maestral is working hard. Inside the park the channels narrow and the wind bends around the islands. It is concentration sailing, the kind our guests end up loving most, and in the wider passages the skipper will happily hand you the wheel.

Where we anchor depends on the wind. Levrnaka has a sand-bottomed bay beneath the tallest cliffs in the park. Ravni Žakan has the konoba we trust. Lavsa gives the quietest night at anchor you will find anywhere on this coast.

Day trip or overnight?

You can touch the edge of the park on a long day of sailing from Zadar, and it is worth doing. But the Kornati keep their best act for the evening. Around five o'clock the excursion boats turn for home, the light goes gold on the limestone, and ninety islands are left to a handful of sailboats. The sea flattens, someone lights a grill on shore, and the silence is the kind you can hear.

That evening is the reason our three-day sailing trip exists: the Kornati on day one, a night at anchor inside Telašćica, then the white sand of Sakarun on the way home. Park entry fees and all meals aboard are included; the konoba dinner is paid in cash and remembered for years.

Practical notes

Bring reef shoes for walking the limestone, a hat, and more water than you think you need. There is no shade ashore. And bring the camera at dusk: Shaw was there in 1929 without one, which might be why he had to reach for tears, stars and breath.
