Kornati Islands from Zadar: What a Sailing Trip Really Looks Like

In 1929, a Nobel-winning playwright stood on a boat somewhere south of Zadar and ran out of clever things to say. George Bernard Shaw, a man rarely lost for words, wrote of what he saw:

"On the last day of the Creation, God desired to crown his work, and thus created the Kornati islands out of tears, stars and breath."George Bernard Shaw, 1929

Skippers quote it to this day, mostly because nobody has managed to put it better since. What follows is the less poetic version: what the Kornati are, who owns them, and what a day of sailing among them is actually like.

What the Kornati islands actually are

The Kornati archipelago counts 89 islands, islets and reefs scattered southwest of Zadar, a national park since 1980. Most of it is bare limestone, streaked silver and gold, dropping into some of the clearest water in the Adriatic. There is no fresh water, no shade and no shop, and that is precisely the point: the Kornati are the Adriatic as it looked before anyone built anything on it.

The islands lie in long parallel rows running northwest to southeast, the same grain that shapes the whole eastern Adriatic. That geometry is why the sailing works here: you slip from one channel into the next, with land on both sides and the swell broken up somewhere behind you.

Look closer at that bare stone and you will see lines running across the islands, from one shore all the way to the other. Those are dry stone walls, about 330 kilometres of them, every metre laid by hand without a drop of mortar. Sheep have grazed here for close to five thousand years, and the walls kept each family's flock on its own ground. The islands are long and narrow, so the walls simply run coast to coast. Nobody built them for the view. They are property lines you can walk along.

The crowns: the side the postcards miss

The outer islands, the ones with their backs to the open sea, hide their best feature from anyone who only comes in from the landward side. Their seaward flanks are sheared off into vertical rock walls that locals call crowns, korone in Croatian. The longest crown above water runs roughly 1,350 metres along the island of Mana. The highest of them all belongs to Klobučar, where the rock stands 82 metres straight up out of deep water.

Sailing under a crown is a strange sensation, because the depth sounder keeps reading numbers that feel wrong for how close the cliff is. There is no shelf here: the rock drops as steeply below the surface as it rises above it, which is also why the diving along this edge is some of the best in the country.

The oddest piece of geology in the park sits below Metlina, at 237 metres the highest point of the Kornati. It is a single vast slab of smooth rock tilted towards the sea, known as Vela Ploča or Magazinova Škrila: around 160 metres long, between 63 and 86 metres wide, roughly 9,100 square metres of bare inclined stone. The likeliest explanation is a landslide after a powerful earthquake, which peeled the hillside open and left the bedding plane exposed like a page torn from a book.

The shepherds who bought an archipelago

Here is the part most day-trippers never hear. In the 1630s, families from Murter and Betina, two towns on an overcrowded little island near Šibenik, began rowing out to the Kornati to tend sheep for the Zadar nobility who owned the pastures. They kept at it for two centuries, out in spring, back in autumn, living in stone huts with cisterns for rainwater.

Then, in the last decades of the 19th century, the shepherds did something remarkable: they bought the islands from their masters. The people of Murter purchased Žut in 1885, and in 1896, together with families from Betina and Zaglav, they bought Kornat and the islands that belong to it. To this day the Kornati are largely owned by Murter families, and the little stone cottages and konobas in the bays belong to their descendants. When you eat grilled fish at a konoba reachable only by boat, your host's family has probably worked that bay for ten generations.

Tureta, and the church built for fishermen

For islands with no permanent population, the Kornati carry a surprising amount of masonry. On Kornat, the largest island, a square fortress called Tureta stands on a ridge above the bay of Tarac. It probably dates from the 6th century, built to watch this stretch of the Adriatic shipping lane in the Byzantine period, and it is the oldest standing building in the park. Below it lie the remains of an early Christian three-nave basilica dedicated to St Mary, with part of the apse and the foundations still readable in the grass.

The other one is easy to sail past without noticing. On Piškera, in the 16th century, there were around forty houses used seasonally by fishermen who came out for the salting season. In 1560 they built themselves a church, fifteen metres by eight, and it is still standing. It is the only church on the Adriatic built specifically for a fishing community rather than a village, which tells you how much fish used to come out of this water.

What the sailing is like

From Zadar you enter the park after two to three hours under sail, most of it in the lee of Dugi Otok, which keeps the sea comfortable even when the maestral is working hard. That afternoon breeze is reliable in summer: it fills in around eleven, builds through the early afternoon and dies at dusk. The mechanics are in our guide to the winds of the Adriatic.

Inside the park the channels narrow and the wind bends around the islands, accelerating through the gaps and going soft in the lees. It is concentration sailing, the kind our guests end up loving most, and in the wider passages the skipper will happily hand you the wheel. Nobody expects you to know anything; if you would rather learn properly, that is what the sailing school is for.

Where we anchor, and why it changes

The honest answer to "which bay" is always "ask on the morning". The park allows anchoring and overnight stays only in a set list of bays, among them Lavsa, Ravni Žakan, Vrulje, Kravljačica, Opat, Smokvica and Anica on Levrnaka, which keeps the pressure off the seagrass everywhere else.

Levrnaka is the one people remember. Its bay of Lojena has the only sandy beach in the whole archipelago, tucked under the island's high side, and the water over that sand does the turquoise trick you normally have to sail much further for. Ravni Žakan has the konoba we trust. Lavsa is a nearly enclosed basin where the only sound after midnight is your own rigging.

Day trip or overnight?

You can touch the edge of the park on a long day of sailing from Zadar, and it is worth doing. But the Kornati keep their best act for the evening. Around five o'clock the excursion boats turn for home, the light goes gold on the limestone, and eighty-nine islands are left to a handful of sailboats. The sea flattens and the silence is the kind you can hear.

That evening is the reason our three-day sailing trip exists: the Kornati on day one, a night at anchor inside Telašćica, then the white sand of Sakarun on the way home. Park entry fees and all meals aboard are included; the konoba dinner is paid in cash and remembered for years. If you have only one day, the day sailing trip stays closer to home among the Zadar islands, which is a different pleasure rather than a lesser one.

Practical notes

Park entry is ticketed and included on our trips, so there is nothing to arrange. Bring reef shoes for the limestone, a hat, and more water than you think you need: no shade ashore, no tap anywhere in the park. Full list in what to bring on a boat trip.

On timing: June and September are the sweet spot, warm water and thin traffic, and the argument for each month is laid out in the best time to sail from Zadar. And bring the camera at dusk. Shaw was there in 1929 without one, which might be why he had to reach for tears, stars and breath.
