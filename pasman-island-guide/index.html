Pašman: Benedictines, and the Channel That Cleans Itself Twice a Day

South of Ugljan and joined to it by a short bridge, Pašman is the island people sail past on their way somewhere else. That is their loss and quietly our gain: it is emptier, greener and, by a measurable margin, cleaner. It covers 60 square kilometres, rises to 272 metres at Veliki Bokolj, and holds a string of villages that still look like villages.

The channel that resets

The Pašman channel, the strip of sea between the island and the mainland, has an unusual habit: its current reverses roughly every six hours. Locals will tell you, with the confidence of people who swim in it daily, that this is why the water here is among the clearest on the Croatian coast, because the channel simply flushes itself twice a day. Whether or not the hydrology is quite that tidy, the swimming is excellent and divers rate the visibility, which is the part that matters when you are in it.

Ždrelac: the bridge that takes masts

Ugljan and Pašman are separated by a gap barely worth the name, spanned since 1972 by the Ždrelac bridge, rebuilt in 2009. Its clearance is 16.5 metres at high tide, which sounds generous until you are standing at the wheel of a boat whose air draft you last checked on a spec sheet. Every summer somebody gets it wrong, and the bridge takes the mast.

The current underneath is the second problem. It reverses with the channel, it is not always predictable in direction, and it can run up to five knots. That is faster than many charter boats motor comfortably, and it means the passage is a question of timing rather than nerve. If you are on a skippered boat with us, this is a non-event. If you are not, look at the tide, look at the mast, and look twice.

Ćokovac: nine centuries of the same routine

On a hill above Tkon stands the monastery of Saints Cosmas and Damian, on a site with an early Christian church and a Byzantine fortification beneath it. Benedictines have been on Ćokovac since the 12th century, arriving after Venice destroyed Biograd on the mainland and the monks moved across the channel to a hill that was easier to defend and harder to be bothered on. It is the only active male Benedictine monastery in Croatia.

It has been burnt, rebuilt, emptied and repopulated, and it was a centre of Glagolitic literacy at a time when writing in the Croatian recension of Church Slavonic was a political act as much as a liturgical one. Monks are there today, keeping the same offices at the same hours their predecessors kept when the Kornati were still grazing land owned by Zadar nobles. Whatever you believe, an institution that has run for nine hundred years on a small island deserves thirty minutes of your day and a quiet voice while you are in it.

The villages, and what they are for

Tkon is the largest and takes the ferry from Biograd. North of it run Kraj, Pašman, Barotul, Mrljane, Neviđane, Dobropoljana, Banj and Ždrelac, most of them strung along the sheltered channel side. They are working places rather than resorts: a bakery, a church, two or three konobas, boats on the hard in winter, olive nets out in November. Nobody here is going to sell you a boat trip on the promenade, because nobody is standing on the promenade.

The bays on the channel side are shallow, sandy and well protected. The seaward side is rougher, less visited, and better if the bura is off. The island also has more forest and cultivated land than most of its neighbours, which is why it stays green through August when the outer islands have gone the colour of a paper bag.

Green, and staying that way

Pašman is the greenest of the inner islands, and the reason is partly geology and partly neglect of the profitable sort. Sixty square kilometres of it rise to the Bokolj ridge, which runs the length of the island and takes the worst of the bura on its seaward flank, leaving the channel side in shelter. That shelter is what the olives, figs and vines have been planted into for a very long time, and the terraces are still cut back rather than left to the macchia. The forest above them is thick enough that the walk up Veliki Bokolj is a genuine hike rather than a scramble over bare stone, and the view from the top runs from Zadar to the Kornati in one sweep.

The island has also stayed off the main tourist circuit, which is largely an accident of ferries: Pašman is served from Biograd rather than Zadar, so the flow of day trippers out of the bigger city goes past it rather than onto it.

The southern approach

Pašman marks the end of the inner chain. South of it the archipelago breaks up into the bare, waterless islands of the Kornati, and the change is abrupt: one moment you are passing olive terraces, the next you are looking at stone with nothing on it.

How Pašman fits a sailing day

The island sits at the far end of our usual cruising ground, which makes it a private charter destination rather than a standard stop: the route bends to your wishes and the day can run long. A realistic plan is Ugljan in the morning, lunch at anchor on the channel side of Pašman, the walk up to Ćokovac if the group is willing, and a long downwind leg home in the afternoon breeze.
