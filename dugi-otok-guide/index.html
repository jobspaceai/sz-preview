Dugi Otok: the Island a Byzantine Emperor Wrote Down as Pizuh

Dugi Otok means "long island", and the name is the least imaginative thing about it. Roughly 45 kilometres from tip to tip and rarely more than four wide, it stands between Zadar and the open Adriatic like a breakwater, and everything worth seeing in this archipelago is either on it, under it, or hidden inside it.

Pizuh: the island enters history

In the middle of the 10th century the Byzantine emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus sat down to write De administrando imperio, a handbook of empire for his son, and listed the islands off the Dalmatian coast. Among them he recorded one under the name Pizuh. That is Dugi Otok's first appearance in writing: not as a holiday destination but as a strategic note in an emperor's manual. A thousand years later the strategy changed hands several times, and the island kept the better view.

What the island holds

Start at the northwestern tip and work south. Sakarun is the white-sand bay everyone photographs, with 350 metres of shallow water and, around the corner, the Veli Rat lighthouse of 1849, the tallest on the Adriatic. Below that, the western coast turns to cliffs and sea caves, one of which an Austro-Hungarian emperor visited in 1904. The eastern coast is quieter: Božava under its pinewood, Brbinj, Savar, Luka, and then Sali, the island's capital and the home of a festival older than fifty years.

At the southern end the sea cuts ten kilometres into the land and creates Telašćica, a nature park with a salt lake, healing mud and cliffs that fall 161 metres. Beyond it lie the Kornati.

The part nobody mentions

Cut into the rock at several points along the coast are tunnels wide enough to swallow a warship. Most were dug during the German occupation in the Second World War, and the Yugoslav navy used them for the next fifty years to hide vessels from satellites and aircraft. Today you can drive a speedboat straight into one. The full story is in our guide to the military tunnels of Dugi Otok.

Getting there, and why the boat matters

Ferries reach Brbinj and Sali, and once ashore you need a car to get anywhere. By boat, the island reads as one continuous coastline: you anchor where the wind suits, swim where the water is best, and skip the parts that are only interesting from a car park. Our speedboat trips take the northern half in a day, Sakarun and the lighthouse included; the three-day sailing route works the southern end, sleeping at anchor in Telašćica.

If you have only one day in the Zadar archipelago and want the postcard version, come here. If you have three, come here slowly.
