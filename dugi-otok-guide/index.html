Dugi Otok: the Island a Byzantine Emperor Wrote Down as Pizuh

Dugi Otok means "long island", and the name is the least imaginative thing about it. It runs 44.5 kilometres from tip to tip, is rarely more than five wide, and stands between Zadar and the open Adriatic like a breakwater. Everything worth seeing in this archipelago is either on it, under it, or hidden inside it. It is also, by a wide margin, the island our guests ask about most, and the one that takes the longest to see properly.

Pizuh: the island enters history

In the middle of the 10th century the Byzantine emperor Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus sat down to write De administrando imperio, a handbook of empire for his son, and listed the islands off the Dalmatian coast. Among them he recorded one under the name Pizuh. That is Dugi Otok's first appearance in writing: not as a holiday destination but as a strategic note in an emperor's manual. Later documents call it Insula Tilagus and then simply Insula maior, the greater island, which is roughly what everyone has called it since.

People were here long before the paperwork. Archaeologists have traced continuous settlement through the Eneolithic, Bronze and Iron Ages, and the population jumped again in the 15th and 16th centuries when Ottoman pressure pushed families off the mainland and onto anything surrounded by water. Most of the villages you sail past today were founded or refounded in that century of running away.

The northwestern end: sand, a lighthouse, and caves

Start at the top and work south. Sakarun is the bay everyone photographs, a shallow crescent where the water runs out roughly 350 metres over pale sand before it gets deep enough to matter. That sand is where the colour comes from, and it is why the bay looks tropical in photographs taken by people standing in knee-deep water.

Around the corner stands the Veli Rat lighthouse, built in 1849 and, at 42 metres, the tallest on the Adriatic. The story locals tell is that a hundred thousand egg yolks went into the ochre plaster to bind it against the salt. Nobody has ever produced the receipt, and nobody on the island seems keen to check.

Just off the point lies a reminder of why the tower is there. The cargo ship Michele ran aground near Veli Rat in 1983 and has been sitting there ever since, broken into two pieces, rusting down a little further each winter. You can swim out to her from the shore, and from a boat she makes the point better than any safety briefing: this is a lee shore with a lighthouse on it, and ships still get it wrong.

Below the lighthouse the western coast turns hostile: high, unbroken cliff facing the open sea, with no shelter for twenty kilometres. Cut into it are the sea caves, of which Golubinka is the one worth the detour, a chamber with a hole in its roof that drops a single column of daylight onto the water. An Austro-Hungarian emperor came to see it in 1904, which tells you the caves were already a known quantity a century before anyone owned a drone.

The eastern coast: the villages that stayed

Turn the corner and the island changes character completely. The eastern shore is low, indented and sheltered, which is why almost every settlement is on this side: Veli Rat, Verunić, Soline, Božava, Dragove, Brbinj, Savar, Luka, Žman, Zaglav and Sali. Between them they hold about 1,655 people, and in February that number is a good deal smaller.

Božava sits under a pine wood with a small harbour and a swimming shelf of smooth rock, and it has been quietly taking visitors since the Austro-Hungarian period. Brbinj and Zaglav take the ferries. Everything in between is a bay with a church and a few houses.

Sali: donkeys, brass and a festival with rules

Sali is the island's capital and its largest village, around 740 people, built around a working fishing harbour rather than a promenade. Since 1959 it has held the Saljske užance on the first weekend of August, a festival that began as an effort to keep the old customs, costumes and food from quietly disappearing.

Out of it came the tovareća mužika, the donkey music: a brass band that plays alongside donkey races, using instruments that trace back to the ox horn once used to coordinate the bluefish catch at night. It was declared an intangible cultural asset of Croatia in 2012. It is loud, deliberately shambolic, and the single least self-important cultural event on this coast.

Telašćica and the southern end

At the southern tip the sea cuts about ten kilometres into the land and creates Telašćica, a nature park with 25 small bays, a salt lake called Mir that runs warmer and saltier than the sea, peloid mud people plaster themselves with, and the Stene cliffs, which drop 161 metres to the water and roughly another 90 below it. Beyond the park the archipelago continues into the Kornati, and the two are usually sailed as one trip because they share a boundary and a weather pattern.

The part nobody mentions

Cut into the rock at several points along the coast are tunnels wide enough to swallow a warship. Most date from the Second World War and the decades after it, and the Yugoslav navy used them to keep vessels out of sight of satellites and aircraft. Today you can take a small boat straight into one, cut the engine, and listen to the water slap around inside a hole somebody drilled for a war that never arrived here. The full story is in our guide to the military tunnels of Dugi Otok.

Getting there, and why the boat matters

Ferries reach Brbinj and Zaglav, and once ashore you need a car, because the island is 44 kilometres long and the road follows the ridge rather than the coast. By boat the island reads as one continuous coastline: you anchor where the wind suits, swim where the water is best, and skip the parts that are only interesting from a car park. Our speedboat trips take the northern half in a day, Sakarun and the lighthouse included; the three-day sailing route works the southern end and sleeps at anchor in Telašćica. For context on how it sits among its neighbours, see the overview of the Zadar archipelago.

If you have one day here and want the postcard version, come to Dugi Otok. If you have three, come slowly, and bring shoes for the cliff path above Mir.
