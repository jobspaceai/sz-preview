Rava and Rivanj: Fifteen Bays, Two Surnames, Thirty-One Residents

Between the big names of the archipelago lie two islands most visitors never learn to pronounce, let alone visit. They have no hotels, no traffic and no plans to acquire either. They are the reason the phrase "off the beaten track" still means something in a part of Europe where it usually does not.

Rava: an island that is mostly coastline

Rava lies in the Iž channel, between Iž and Dugi Otok, and covers around seven square kilometres. Into that it fits fifteen bays: Tanko, Vališina, Ivanoševica, Pavajsko, Pestehovac, Dražice, Golubovac, Martinica, Grbavač, Grbačina, Marnjica, Paladinjica, Vićabok, Lokvina and Za Grbicu.

The list tells you what the island is. Rava is a low, indented ridge of stone and pine where the sea reaches into the land from both sides and nothing is ever more than a few minutes' walk from water. The settlement is split between two harbours, Vela Rava and Mala Rava, and the population you could seat comfortably in a village bar with room for their visitors.

For a boat, this ratio is close to ideal. Fifteen bays on an island seven kilometres square means that whatever the wind is doing, one of them is calm and most of them are empty. It also means that if the forecast changes at four in the afternoon, you move around the corner rather than making a two-hour crossing.

Rava is also, nominally, part of a kingdom. Every 29 July the village of Veli Iž elects a King of Iž, Rava and Lavdara for a one-year term, a medieval custom that has survived on these three pieces of land and nowhere else on the coast. The details are in our guide to Iž and its potters.

Rivanj: thirty-one people and two surnames

Rivanj is smaller still: 4.4 square kilometres, 3.4 kilometres long, up to 1.4 wide, with a high point called Lukocina at 112 metres. Thirty-one people were counted in 2011 and the number has not gone up since.

It first appears in written sources in the middle of the 14th century, as Rivan or Ripanium, and it was part of the Zadar commune. The present village was founded in the early 16th century by settlers from Ugljan, and by 1640 the whole island had been bought by the Lantana family, Zadar nobility. The settlers stayed and the nobles did not, which is why there are exactly two family surnames on Rivanj today, Radulić and Fatović, and one church, St Jelena.

Like most of these islands, the village sits inland on the high ground, with a small harbour and a handful of houses below it on the southwestern shore. Sheep still graze the terraces. In winter the population is a few families; in August it is a few families and their visiting grandchildren.

What is offshore

Rivanj matters to us for what lies just off it. The waters between here and Sestrunj are shallow, protected and clear, and this is where the Tri Sestrice islets sit: the first swim stop of our full-day route, and the one that makes people rethink their opinion of snorkelling.

The submarine landscape around Rivanj is rich enough that divers come specifically for it, which is unusual for an island with no dive centre, no shop and no road worth the name. A little further west, off Sestrunj, the cargo ship Ledenik went down in 2008 and now lies shallow enough to snorkel, which makes this stretch of water the one part of the archipelago where you can see a wreck without a tank on your back.

The other small ones

Rava and Rivanj are not alone. The archipelago has a whole outer rank of small inhabited islands that most itineraries skip: Sestrunj with 48 residents and no cafe, Zverinac with fewer, Lavdara with none permanently, and further out Silba, Olib and Ist, each with a summer population many times its winter one. Silba is the best known of them, because it banned cars decades ago and has a sand beach that gets photographed.

They also share a demographic curve that goes one way. Almost all of them peaked between the wars and have been losing people since, and the ones under fifty residents have already lost their schools. The summer population papers over it: in August a village of thirty looks like a village of two hundred, because everyone who left is back for three weeks. In November the number on the census is the number on the island.

What they share is the thing that makes them worth the detour: nobody is performing for you. There is no waterfront strip, no boat selling tickets, and no queue for anything, because there is nothing to queue for.

Why bother

Because this is where the archipelago stops putting on a show. On Dugi Otok in August you share a beach; here you share nothing but the channel with a fishing boat. The trade is straightforward: you give up shops, restaurants and certainty about lunch, and you get water nobody else is in.

If you have a boat for the day and want to spend it somewhere that will not appear on anyone's feed, point at Rava and see what happens. The full-day route passes through this water as standard, and on a private charter the skipper will happily spend the afternoon in one of those fifteen bays rather than crossing to somewhere with a name you already know.
