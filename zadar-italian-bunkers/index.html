The Concrete Ring: Zadar's Italian Bunkers and Tunnels, 1920 to 1943

Zadar spent twenty three years as an Italian town with an international border running through its own back gardens. The administration that ran it did not trust that border for a minute, so it poured concrete: gun positions, command posts and tunnels cut into the karst in a ring around the city. Roughly 250 of them are still out there, and one has been restored and opened. It is the most unusual thing you can go and look at in Zadar, and hardly anyone does.

The city that became an island without moving

The Treaty of Rapallo, signed on 12 November 1920, gave Zadar to Italy and everything behind it to the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes. What Italy actually received was about 52 square kilometres: the town plus the cadastral municipalities of Arbanasi (Borgo Erizzo), Crno (Cerno), Bokanjac (Boccagnazzo) and part of Diklo. From Bibinje to Diklo, and inland only as far as the Bokanjac marsh.

That is a very small country. The border began at the sea about 700 metres southeast of Diklo and cut inland toward a hill marked as point 66. In practice a farmer could own an olive grove in one state and a house in another, and the road to the hinterland stopped at a customs post a twenty minute walk from the cathedral.

A port cut off from its own hinterland has two options: make peace with the neighbours, or dig in. Between the wars, Zadar dug in.

A ring of concrete around 52 square kilometres

Construction began in earnest around 1931 and ran through the decade. It was not a line but a semicircle, three to five belts deep depending on the sector. The names on the map are ordinary Zadar suburbs: Bokanjac, Musapstan, Žmirići, Ploče, Diklo, Ćubrijan, Crno, Gaženica, Bibinje. Places people now drive through on the way to a supermarket.

The forward positions were the big ones, oval underground works with very thick walls and domed roofs. Behind them came smaller shelters simply dug into the ground, plus telephone posts, ammunition stores and connecting tunnels, some reaching about twenty metres down.

The person who finally counted them is Jurica Vučetić, a Zadar researcher who spent years walking the terrain, photographing and drawing what he found. His catalogue runs to roughly 250 bunkers and tunnel structures across the wider Zadar area, and he has said Google Earth did a good part of the work for him, because once you know what a concrete dome looks like from above you start seeing them everywhere. City sources sometimes quote around 260, which tells you how precise any such count can be.

What is actually inside one

The complex under Bokanjac is the best described, and it is worth knowing what to expect before you imagine something cinematic. Sleeping quarters for the garrison, a command room labelled Comando di Compagnia, a telephone post (Posto telefono), a radio post (Posto Radio), and a latrine with two concrete fixtures and no privacy whatsoever.

Air came in through hand cranked ventilators fitted with filters, which tells you what the builders expected and did not want to write on the plans. Stencilled Italian lettering is still legible on some walls, and so is Croatian graffiti from the 1990s in the same rooms, which is the fastest history lesson available in this city.

One catalogued work, listed under the name Fratelli Croce, has four entrances and more than a hundred metres of corridor running almost entirely beneath a cemetery. The stairs down to it start between two graves.

The Venetian bastions got a second job

The most surprising part of the network is not new construction at all. The Renaissance bastions Venice raised against the Ottomans, now inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list, were hollowed out and converted into air raid shelters under the Italian administration. One of them, a command shelter named after Enrico Toti, sits inside the old ramparts with four entrances and three rooms.

So the walls you walk through at the Land Gate were doing military work in 1543 and again in 1943, against a threat their builders could not have pictured. Nothing on the outside tells you this. That is rather the point of a shelter.

1943: the ring was pointing the wrong way

Italy left the war in September 1943 and German forces took over the town within days. Then the bombers came, and the whole elaborate landward defence turned out to be irrelevant, because the threat arrived from above. Between November 1943 and October 1944 Allied raids destroyed roughly four fifths of Zadar. The bunkers built to stop an army walking in from Bokanjac did nothing at all, while the shelters cut into the bastions carried the civilian population through the worst of it. We cover that year in our piece on underground Zadar and the winter of 1943.

The ring then had a third career. In the war of 1991 to 1995 the same structures were used again by Croatian soldiers holding the line above the city, with beds installed in bunkers empty for half a century. Some Bokanjac residents played in those tunnels as children and sheltered in them as adults.

Locally the three chapters get compressed into one sentence: the Italians dug them, the Germans used them, and the Croatian Army defended the city from them. It sounds like a line, and it happens to be accurate. The Italian engineers did not put the ring on the border itself but further inland, where the sightlines were better, and half a century later that choice meant the positions were still in the right places. The Germans in 1943 used them mainly for observation. In 1991 local crisis staffs cleared out structures the city had forgotten, and they turned out to matter in the defence of the wider Zadar area.

The person who knows most about them is Jurica Vučetić, a lawyer who has spent years cataloguing them, partly from satellite imagery. The professional term for this kind of thing is unwanted heritage: nobody commissioned them as monuments, nobody is fond of them, and they cannot be removed either. The Zadar architects' society held a public discussion on exactly that in 2019, inside the same project that later restored C-47.

C-47 above Vidikovac: the one you can walk into

For decades all of this sat abandoned. Then the Interreg Italy-Croatia project REVIVAL put money into it: about 388,640 euros for Zadar alone, inside a project worth roughly 1.8 million across all partners. The site chosen was the underground fort catalogued as C-47, in the Vidikovac area on the high ground north of the old town.

Restoration finished in 2021. The design, by Ante Šime Andrović and his team, treats about 500 square metres of fortification on an 8,000 square metre site as a public lookout rather than a museum: a 180 metre concrete walkway threading between the existing pines, benches at intervals, and fluorescent pebbles set into the path that soak up daylight and glow after dark instead of competing with the city lights below. The interior is set up for small exhibitions and events.

The view is the reason to go even if the concrete leaves you cold: the whole city laid out, the Velebit ridge behind it, the channel and the islands in front. In December 2021 a driving route was presented linking C-47 with the bunkers at Musapstan, Crno, Križ and Dračevac. Opening hours for the interior have been erratic since, so ask at the tourist office before you climb up. The walkway and the viewpoint are always open.

The other 249, and why any of this belongs in a sailing guide

Most of the network is not visitable and should not be entered. Passages flood after heavy rain, some are unstable, several entrances have been buried by later building work, and a surprising number sit in private gardens with the family car parked on the roof. What you can do, easily, is spot them. Once you have seen one concrete dome in a field near Crno you will not stop seeing them.

It belongs here because it completes the picture. Zadar reads as a soft, sunlit place from the deck of a boat, and it is, but everything you can see from the water was fortified by somebody: the Venetian walls on the peninsula, Fort St Michael on the ridge of Ugljan opposite, and a buried ring behind the town that you cannot see at all. Come back into the harbour at dusk on our sunset sail, or spend the day among the islands on the full day trip and look back at the coast on the way in. It stops being a skyline and becomes a border that was drawn in the wrong place and defended anyway.
