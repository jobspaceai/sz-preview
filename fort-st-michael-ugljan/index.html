Fort St Michael: the 13th-Century Watchtower With a View of 200 Islands

There is a hill above the village of Preko on Ugljan with a ruined fortress on top, and from up there you can see more than two hundred islands. On a clear day the view runs from the Velebit mountains on the mainland to the outer edge of the Kornati, with the whole of Zadar laid out across the channel like a model of itself. It is the best vantage point in the archipelago, and people have understood that for about fifteen hundred years.

Why anyone built here

Position, and nothing else. Ugljan is a long, thin island lying parallel to the coast, and this hill is its high point, sitting almost exactly opposite Zadar. From up here you see every approach to the city: the northern channel past Rivanj and Sestrunj, the southern one past Pašman, and the open gap to the west where anything coming from Italy has to appear. Nothing crosses that water without being seen from this hill, in either direction.

The earliest fortification here is credited to the Byzantines in the 6th century, when this coast was the eastern empire's maritime frontier and Zadar was its regional capital. Whatever they built is long gone. What you see now is medieval and mostly Venetian: the Republic of Venice rebuilt the fort in 1202, the year the Fourth Crusade was diverted to sack Zadar on Venice's behalf, and strengthened it through the 13th century.

The reasoning was not subtle. Venice wanted to control Zadar, discourage the city from rebuilding its own defences, and protect its shipping lanes. A fortress on the opposite hilltop did all three at once.

The monks got there first

Before the castle there was a Benedictine monastery of St Michael on or near the site, documented somewhere between the late 11th and the mid 12th century, and it is the monastery that gave the hill and the fortress their name. The Benedictines were doing on Ugljan what they did across the Adriatic: farming, pressing oil, keeping records, and holding a piece of high ground that turned out to be militarily useful to somebody else later.

Olive terraces still climb most of the way up the slope, and the drystone walls holding them in place are the same technology, laid by the same kind of patient labour, as the walls of the fort above.

The sieges

The hill earned its keep during the Venetian siege of Zadar in 1345 and 1346, the campaign recorded in the medieval chronicle known as the Obsidio Iadrensis. Zadar had rebelled against Venice, again, and Venice besieged it by land and sea, again. Whoever held this fortress could watch every ship entering or leaving the harbour and interfere with most of them. The city fell in December 1346.

Afterwards the fort kept working as a signal and observation post through the Ottoman centuries, when a fire lit on this summit was the fastest way to tell the islands that something was wrong.

What is up there now

Ruins, a small chapel, a bell, a modern telecommunications mast on the neighbouring hill, and the view. Parts of the walls are standing, the interior is open to the sky and carries a certain amount of graffiti, and the site is free to enter. It is a protected cultural monument, which in practice means it is being looked after slowly rather than restored quickly.

The walk up from Preko takes around an hour on a marked path through macchia and olive terraces, climbing all 265 metres from the waterfront. You can also drive most of the way and walk the last stretch. Go early or late: there is no shade at the top, the stone holds the afternoon heat, and the light for photographs is better at either end of the day anyway. Take water. There is none up there.

What you can actually see

More than two hundred islands, which sounds like a marketing number until you are standing there trying to count them. In the foreground, Preko and the wooded islet of Galevac, then Ošljak, the smallest inhabited island in the Adriatic. Behind them, Zadar on its peninsula with the cathedral tower visible in decent light. To the north, Rivanj, Sestrunj and the long back of Dugi Otok. To the south, Pašman and the channel toward the Kornati. Behind the mainland, the Velebit ridge, which is where the bura comes from.

What the view really shows you is why the archipelago works the way it does: parallel islands, parallel channels, and shelter in almost any wind if you pick the right side of the right island. That is the whole logic of sailing here, visible in one glance.

How it fits a day on the water

From a boat, Fort St Michael is the landmark you steer by. It is the notch on Ugljan's ridge that tells you where Preko is, and once you can pick it out you can place yourself in the channel without looking at anything else. Our day sailing route passes below it heading out, and the half-day trip swims at Galevac and Ošljak, the islets sitting directly under the hill.

If you have a spare morning on land before or after your sail, the ferry from Zadar to Preko takes about twenty minutes and runs frequently. Climb the hill, then go sailing, and spend the rest of the day recognising from sea level every island you looked down on that morning. It is the cheapest way we know of to make an archipelago make sense.
