The Sea Organ in Zadar: 35 Pipes, Seven Chords, and Hitchcock’s Favourite Sunset

In May 1964, Alfred Hitchcock checked into room 204 of the Hotel Zagreb on Zadar's waterfront. The master of suspense spent his evenings watching the sun go down over the channel, and left the city with a line the tourist board has been quoting ever since:

"Zadar has the most beautiful sunset in the world, more beautiful than the one in Key West, Florida, applauded at every evening."Alfred Hitchcock, Zadar, May 1964

He saw it from a hotel window. You can do better.

What was there before

The stretch of waterfront the Sea Organ occupies was, for most of the second half of the 20th century, one of the least loved places in the city. Zadar was bombed heavily in the Second World War and lost around four fifths of its buildings, and the rebuilt harbour front was a functional concrete quay for ferries, with a wall you could not sit on and nothing to look at except the ferries themselves. People walked along it. Nobody stopped.

That is the context for what happened in 2005, and it is why locals talk about the project the way they do. It did not decorate a beautiful place. It rescued an ugly one.

An instrument played by the sea

Forty-one years after Hitchcock's visit, on 15 April 2005, the architect Nikola Bašić opened a rebuilt stretch of that same waterfront with an instrument hidden inside it. Under a run of wide white marble steps descending into the sea sit 35 organ pipes, spread along about seventy metres of shoreline. Waves push water into channels beneath the steps; the water displaces air; the air is forced up through the pipes and out through narrow slots cut into the promenade above.

The pipes are tuned in seven groups of five, following chord sequences drawn from klapa, the Dalmatian close-harmony singing tradition. That is the detail that makes the whole thing work. Whatever the sea plays is always, somehow, in harmony, because the instrument cannot produce a note that does not belong.

The result is the Morske orgulje, the Sea Organ: a musical instrument the size of a waterfront, playing continuously since the day it opened, with no power supply and no off switch. A calm sea produces long, meditative chords with gaps between them. A ferry's wake sets off a sudden chorus that runs down the steps as the wave passes. A strong southerly and the thing roars. No two minutes are ever the same, which is why people who planned to stay five minutes are still sitting there an hour later.

In 2006 the project shared the European Prize for Urban Public Space, an award that goes to interventions in public space rather than buildings, which is exactly the right category. It turned a plain concrete shoreline into the most loved few metres of the city.

The Greeting to the Sun, next door

Three years later, in 2008, Bašić finished the second half of the idea a few dozen metres along the promenade. The Greeting to the Sun is a circle 22 metres across, set flush into the stone pavement, made of three hundred multi-layered glass plates with photovoltaic modules underneath. During the day it collects sunlight. After dark it plays it back as a light show under your feet, and it feeds part of the electricity for the waterfront lighting back into the grid.

The pairing is the point: one installation takes its energy from the waves and gives you sound, the other takes it from the sun and gives you light, and they run on the same stretch of pavement. Children discover the second one within about four seconds of arriving and stay until dragged away.

When to go, and the problem with going

Sunset, obviously. The afternoon maestral usually gives the organ its liveliest voice just as the light turns gold, because that breeze builds a small steady swell that arrives at the steps at a useful rhythm. In midsummer the sun goes down after eight; in September it is closer to seven, which is a far more civilised dinner schedule.

The geography helps too. Zadar looks west down a channel with the island of Ugljan on the far side, so the sun sets into a gap between land and land rather than over open horizon. That is what gives the light its colour: it comes across water, low, with island silhouettes at either edge of the frame. Hitchcock was not being generous. He was describing a specific optical situation that happens to occur here almost every clear evening from May to October.

That is also the problem: in July and August the steps are packed shoulder to shoulder, and the world's most beautiful sunset comes with an audience of several hundred people, phones raised. Come at six in the morning and the instrument is yours alone, with the added benefit that the night ferries have just been through and the water is still moving. Or do what locals with boats do.

The version almost nobody hears

From the water, thirty metres off the steps, the Sea Organ is a different instrument. The sound reaches you rounder and quieter, mixed with the small noises of the city behind it, and the crowd on the steps becomes part of the view instead of your seat neighbours. You also get the geometry right for the first time: the peninsula in profile, the bell tower behind, the light coming off the stone.

Our evening sail idles past the organ exactly at golden hour, then turns west into the channel. The sun drops behind Ugljan, there is local wine in your glass, and somewhere behind you the sea keeps playing to an audience that never claps, because the organ never stops for applause.

If you would rather see the whole peninsula from the water in daylight, with the Roman forum, St Donatus and the walls in the right order, that is the old town from the sea, and it works well as a half-day sail. The city has one more layer worth knowing about before you go, underground and considerably less romantic, in Zadar's wartime tunnels.

Hitchcock had room 204. You will have the whole channel.
