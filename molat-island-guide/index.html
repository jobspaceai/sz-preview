Molat: Three Villages That Turned Their Backs on the Sea

Islands are supposed to face the sea. Molat does not. Its three settlements, Molat, Zapuntel and Brgulje, all sit a careful distance inland, and the reason is not shyness. It is piracy.

A defensive habit that lasted centuries

Molat has been inhabited since the Stone Age and first appears in written records in the 10th century. For much of the period that followed, the open Adriatic on the island's western side was a working corridor for raiders, and a village visible from a passing sail was a village worth visiting. So the islanders farmed, kept sheep, fished, and put their houses where the smoke would not give them away. The bays stayed empty and the villages stayed inland, and the pattern held long enough to become simply how Molat is built.

What that leaves for a boat

It leaves a coastline of empty coves. Because nobody built along the water, Molat's bays are still what the rest of the coast looked like fifty years ago: pine, stone, clear water, and nothing else. The island covers around 23 square kilometres, and the population still supports itself on agriculture, sheep, fishing and, increasingly, sailors who anchor for the night.

Molat sits well out toward the open sea, roughly on the line between Dugi Otok and Premuda. That distance is why it stays quiet, and why reaching it is a speedboat job or a full private day rather than a casual afternoon.

Going

Our speedboat trips can include Molat on a longer route; on a multi-day sail it is a natural overnight stop on the way out or back. Bring what you need with you: there are shops, but this is not an island that expects you.
