Molat: Three Villages That Turned Their Backs on the Sea

Islands are supposed to face the sea. Molat does not. Its three settlements, Molat, Zapuntel and Brgulje, all sit a careful distance inland, and the reason is not shyness. It is four hundred years of people arriving by boat with bad intentions.

A defensive habit that lasted centuries

Molat appears in writing around 950, in the same imperial handbook that recorded Dugi Otok, and the name Melata turns up in documents from 1073. For much of the period that followed, the open Adriatic on the island's western side was a working corridor for raiders, and a village visible from a passing sail was a village worth visiting. So the islanders farmed, kept sheep, fished, and put their houses where the smoke would not give them away.

The pattern held long enough to stop being a defensive measure and simply become how Molat is built. Molat village holds about 107 people, Brgulje 48 and Zapuntel 42. All three look inward. Their harbours are separate places, a walk away, used for boats rather than lived in.

The bay at Jazi

There is a harder chapter, and it belongs on any honest page about this island.

On 30 June 1942 the Italian occupation authorities opened an internment camp in the bay of Jazi on Molat. It ran until 8 September 1943. It held mainly Croatian and Slovene civilians from the surrounding islands and coast, and the majority of the inmates were women, children and the elderly, taken as relatives of people the occupiers considered a political threat. The camp consisted of five barracks. Estimates of the number of people who passed through it range from about 20,000 to 25,000, and between roughly 500 and 1,000 of them died there of hunger, disease and exposure, or were shot.

When news of the Italian armistice reached the island in September 1943, the guards left. A group of inmates disarmed the remaining soldiers, and Partisan boats evacuated most of the surviving prisoners to the mainland before German troops reached Molat. There is a memorial at the site. If you anchor in the bay, it takes ten minutes to walk to it, and it is worth those ten minutes.

What is left of the population

Molat had 1,083 residents in 1937 and has fewer than 200 now. The school, opened in 1880, has closed for lack of children. The reasons are the ordinary ones across these islands, emigration and the pull of the mainland, but the war did not help, and the numbers never came back.

What is there now is agriculture, sheep, fishing, a summer population that multiplies the winter one several times over, and a growing number of boats that anchor for the night. The island is also recognised as an Important Bird Area for its breeding seabirds, which is a polite way of saying that large parts of it have been left alone.

What that leaves for a boat

It leaves a coastline of empty coves. Because nobody built along the water, Molat's bays are still what most of this coast looked like fifty years ago: pine to the waterline, stone, clear water, and nothing else. There is no marina to speak of and no promenade to walk along in the evening, which filters the traffic more effectively than any regulation.

The island sits well out toward the open sea, on the line between Dugi Otok and Premuda, with Ist, Škarda and Silba as its neighbours to the north. That distance is why it stays quiet, and why reaching it is a speedboat job or a full private day rather than a casual afternoon.

What there is to do, which is not much

Molat is not an island with attractions. There is a walk between the three villages along old paths and a road that sees a handful of vehicles a day. There are olive groves and sheep pasture behind dry stone walls that have been maintained for so long that nobody knows who built them. There is a beach at Jazi and a string of smaller ones you reach only from the water. There are konobas in the villages that open when there are people to feed, and a harbour at Zapuntel that is one of the better-protected anchorages in the outer archipelago.

The point of coming here is the absence of things. If you have already done Sakarun and the Telašćica circuit and want to see what the same coast looks like without a car park at the end of it, this is the answer. It is also, for the same reason, a bad choice for a first day out with people who want lunch at one o'clock in a specific place.

Going

Our speedboat trips can include Molat on a longer route; on a multi-day sail it is a natural overnight stop on the way out or back, particularly if the forecast makes the outer islands sensible. Check the wind first: this is the part of the archipelago where the bura arrives with the least warning, and our guide to the Adriatic winds explains why.

Bring what you need with you. There are shops, in the sense that a shop exists, but this is not an island that expects you, and nothing here runs on a timetable designed for visitors.
