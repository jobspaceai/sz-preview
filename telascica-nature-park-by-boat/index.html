Telašćica: Cliffs, a Salt Lake With Healing Mud, and the Safest Bay in the Adriatic

In this article

  2. The lake that heals
  3. The cliffs
  4. Donkeys, olives and five hundred kinds of green
  5. Getting there, and staying

Telašćica is a trick of geography. At the southern end of Dugi Otok, the sea cuts ten kilometres into the island, forming a bay so sheltered that nautical charts mark it as a natural harbour safe from every wind. Sailors have been hiding here for two thousand years. The name itself is medieval and comes from "tri jezera", three lakes, which is roughly how the bay's three linked basins look from the hills above. Since 1988 the whole thing has been a protected nature park of about seventy square kilometres, half of it sea.

What makes it worth a whole day rather than a swim stop is that the park stacks three completely different landscapes within twenty minutes of each other: a sheltered inland sea, an open-ocean cliff, and a salt lake with mud people queue for.

The bay that never gets rough

Telašćica works as a harbour because of its shape. The entrance faces northwest, the bay bends, and then it branches into arms that face different directions, so whichever way the wind is blowing there is an arm lying quietly in the lee of a hill. The bura can be howling across the top of the island while the water below is flat enough to see the anchor.

The park maintains mooring buoys in several of those arms and charges a daily fee that includes entry. Boats are not permitted to anchor freely everywhere, which is the reason the seagrass meadows on the bottom are still in the condition they are. Twenty-five islands and islets belong to the park, most of them small enough to walk around in half an hour.

From the anchorage at Mir the walk to the lake takes about ten minutes on a gravel track, and the climb to the cliff viewpoint roughly twenty more, uphill through low scrub with no shade at any point. That is the whole logistics problem of Telašćica. Everything is close, nothing is difficult, and the only thing that will hurt you is the sun. Take water and go in the morning or the late afternoon.

The lake that heals

Behind the beach at Mir lies the park's strangest feature: a salt lake, about 900 metres long and 300 wide, that the open sea feeds through cracks in the limestone. After the last Ice Age the sea rose and flooded a karst depression through those underground fissures, and the lake has been there ever since. Because it is shallow, never more than six metres, it warms faster than the sea and stays saltier, which means you float in it almost without trying. The name Mir means "peace", and early in the morning, when the only sound is donkeys arguing somewhere in the olives, it earns it.

At the lake's southwestern end there is one more surprise: deposits of peloid, a dark healing mud that locals have smeared on themselves for generations against rheumatic and skin complaints. You will see visitors standing in the shallows painted grey from neck to knee, waiting for the mud to dry. It costs nothing and washes off in the lake. The water also holds an isolated population of eels, cut off from the sea and known locally as kajman, which is a lot of biology for a puddle the size of a few football pitches.

The cliffs

Walk twenty minutes uphill from the bay and Dugi Otok simply ends. The cliffs known as Stene fall from the Grpašćak viewpoint, 161 metres above the sea, straight down into open water, and they keep going: the rock face continues about 90 metres below the surface. Peregrine falcons nest in the wall.

The viewpoint at the top is not a modern platform. It is a small Austro-Hungarian fortification, built to watch the approaches to this coast and now converted into a visitor point with an exhibition inside. On a clear day the horizon to the west is Italy. Sunset from up there, with the Kornati scattered south, is the finest view in the Zadar archipelago, and most day-boat visitors never have time to see it, which is the strongest single argument for sleeping in the bay.

Donkeys, olives and five hundred kinds of green

The park counts more than five hundred plant species, along with corals and rare algae in its waters. It also has donkeys. There are currently around fourteen of them, Dalmatian donkeys of the native breed, and they wander the bay as if they had signed the founding charter. They are friendly, photogenic and entirely uninterested in your schedule. Do not feed them bread; the rangers will tell you the same thing, more politely.

Ashore, the terraces around the bay are still worked by families from Sali, the biggest village on Dugi Otok and the closest thing the island has to a capital. Sali has its own reason to be visited, mostly involving brass instruments and donkeys again, which we cover in the guide to Sali.

Getting there, and staying

There is no good way to see Telašćica by car. You would need a ferry, a long drive down the spine of the island and a walk, and you would arrive at the same hour as everyone else. The bay rewards boats. From Zadar it pairs naturally with the Kornati, since the park border is a few hundred metres of water, and the two parks together make one of the best two-day routes in Croatia.

Once the excursion traffic leaves in the late afternoon, Telašćica goes so quiet you can hear fish break the surface. On our three-day sailing route this is where we spend the second night, at anchor, after a day in the Kornati and before the morning at Sakarun. Guests consistently name it the night they remember longest.

If three days is more than your holiday allows, the fast option is a speedboat trip, which reaches the bay in a fraction of the time and gets you back to Zadar for dinner. It is a different trip, not a worse one, and it is the right call if you are short on days. The rest of the island, including the caves along the western shore and the tunnels the Yugoslav navy cut into the rock, is covered in our guide to Dugi Otok.
