Sali: an Island Capital With a Donkey Orchestra

At the southern end of Dugi Otok, a few minutes by sea from the entrance to Telašćica, sits Sali: the island's largest settlement, its administrative centre, and for most of a thousand years a fishing town that lived off sardines. Around 726 people live in the town itself and roughly 1,750 in the municipality, which covers twelve settlements over 124 square kilometres. It is a working place with a deep, well-sheltered harbour, and for one weekend each August it turns into something no tourist board could have designed.

A thousand years of sardines

The oldest written record of fishing in Croatia, dated to around 995, concerns the fishing grounds around Sali. That is not a local boast, it is the document historians actually cite, and it tells you what this place has always been about. The waters south of Dugi Otok, where the deep channel meets the shallows around the Kornati, hold sardines, and Sali is the harbour with the best access to them.

For most of that millennium the catch was salted. Fishermen worked at night with lamps, the fish went into barrels with salt, and the barrels went to Venice. The name of the town is usually connected to salt, which fits the trade if not necessarily the etymology.

The factory that outlasted thirty others

In 1905 the Mardešić family opened a fish canning factory in Sali, and it is still running. That makes it the oldest island cannery in Croatia and one of only two sardine factories left on any Croatian island. There were once thirty-two. The rest closed as the industry consolidated onto the mainland, and what kept this one alive was partly stubbornness and partly the fact that the fish are still there.

It matters more than it sounds. A working factory means a working fleet, and a working fleet means Sali still has a reason to exist in February. Plenty of Adriatic island towns do not, and you can tell the difference within about ten minutes of stepping ashore.

Saljske užance

The festival has been running since 1958: three days of food, sport, music and local ceremony, held on the weekend before Velika Gospa, the Assumption on 15 August, so usually the second weekend of the month. Check the dates before you plan around it, because they move with the calendar.

It opens with the fishing boats coming in, flags going up, and the whole town eating. There is a competition for the best brudet, the Dalmatian fish stew, tuna and sardines going on grills along the waterfront, klapa singing after dark, and a programme of sporting events with one clear headline act: trka tovarov, the donkey race.

The donkey orchestra

The centrepiece, though, is the tovareća mužika, founded in 1959 and now the festival's emblem. A brass band parades through the town accompanied by donkeys, and the whole thing began as a parody of formal municipal band music: the joke being that the donkeys keep better time than the players. Sixty-odd years later it has stopped being a parody of anything and become the real institution, known well beyond Dugi Otok.

It is loud, deliberately shambolic and entirely unlike a festival designed for visitors, because it was not designed for visitors. The traditional finish is the musicians ending up in the harbour, instruments and all. Nobody seems to mind.

The rest of the year

Sali outside August is quiet in the best way. A harbour full of working boats, a few konobas, a small supermarket, a shipyard at nearby Sašćica, olive groves on the slopes behind, and one of the finest nature parks in the country a short run away by sea. The harbour is a genuine all-weather shelter, which is why sailors have used it for centuries and why it remains one of the better overnight stops in the southern archipelago. There is water and power on the town quay, and a proper konoba dinner at the end of it.

September in Sali is close to perfect: warm sea, no crowds, and a town that has gone back to doing what it does.

Telašćica, next door

Sali is the gateway to Telašćica Nature Park, and most people who stop here are on their way in or out of it. The bay cuts about ten kilometres into the island and holds around twenty-five smaller bays inside it, plus the salt lake of Mir and, on the seaward side, the Stene cliffs that drop 161 metres straight into the Adriatic and continue another ninety below the surface.

Getting there by boat

Sali sits at the far end of our cruising ground, which makes it a stop on the three-day sailing route rather than a day trip. We come past it heading in or out of Telašćica, and it is the natural place to tie up for a night with a restaurant at the end of the passerelle. On a fast boat it is reachable in a day from Zadar, but you will spend most of that day moving rather than swimming.

If your dates land on the festival weekend, tell us when you book. Lying at anchor near a town with a donkey orchestra in full swing is not an experience you can schedule twice, and the anchorages around Sali fill up early that weekend.
