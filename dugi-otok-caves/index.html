The Caves of Dugi Otok: an Emperor’s Excursion and a Hole in the Ceiling

In this article

The western coast of Dugi Otok faces the open Adriatic and takes the full weight of every storm that crosses it. A few thousand years of that produces cliffs, and cliffs in limestone produce caves. Two of them are worth planning a day around, and one of them was a tourist attraction before tourism existed here in any organised form.

Strašna peć: the cave with imperial credentials

Near the village of Savar, in the central part of the island, is a cave called Strašna peć. The name translates as something close to the terrible cave, which tells you what the locals thought of it before anyone started selling tickets.

It sits about seventy metres above sea level, cut into well-layered rudist limestone. You go in through an opening created when part of the vault collapsed, and the passage descends gently at first and then increasingly steeply toward a floor filled with fallen blocks. Inside there are the usual limestone decorations, stalactites and columns, and the acoustics that go with a large sealed chamber in rock.

How a hole in the rock became a destination

The written history starts in 1898, when Dr Luka Jelić described the cave in the Zadar newspaper Il Dalmata. In 1904 the Illustrierte österreichische Riviera Zeitung published on it, and visitors began arriving in numbers. That makes Strašna peć the first organised excursion destination not just on Dugi Otok but in this part of the Adriatic generally.

The same year, the Austro-Hungarian emperor Franz Joseph came to see it. He was impressed enough to pay for improvements: iron doors at the entrance, two lanterns inside, and a proper staircase up from the sea so visitors could arrive by boat and climb to the mouth without scrambling.

Think about what that means in sequence. Before Sakarun, before the national park, before anyone had heard of the Kornati, before any of the things people now come to this coast for, the thing on this island worth showing an emperor was a hole in the rock. Tourism on Dugi Otok started here, in 1904, with a staircase and two lamps.

Golubinka: the light through the roof

Golubinka is on the southwestern coast, near the bay of Brbinjšćica, and it works on a completely different principle. The entrance is above the waterline and the cave extends both above and below the sea, but the reason people come is overhead: an opening in the ceiling that drops a shaft of daylight straight down into the chamber and turns the water underneath a colour that does not photograph properly and looks fake when it does.

The cave is also a serious piece of habitat. It holds the largest bat colony on the island, over three thousand animals, and the largest recorded sponge habitat, with around fifty different species. That is worth knowing before you shout inside.

Access is the interesting part. The safe way in is by kayak in a settled sea, in an organised group, with no wind and no swell. The other way, which the bolder do, is to jump from the rock above and swim in through a narrow passage. The swim from the nearest sensible landing runs to roughly seven hundred metres. Timing matters: the light does its trick in the afternoon, when the sun is far enough round to come through the roof opening at the right angle.

Brbinjšćica: the bay under the cliffs

On the same western shore lies Brbinjšćica, a bay ringed by high cliffs with more caves scattered along them. It is a location of recognised geomorphological significance, which is the official way of saying that the rock here does something unusual.

It is exposed, so it needs a settled day. On that day it is one of the most dramatic anchorages in the archipelago: sheer rock on three sides, deep blue water, nothing built, nothing sold, nobody there. This is the coast that faces Italy with nothing in between, and it looks like it.

The weather problem

All three of these are boat-only and all three need calm weather, and the western coast is unforgiving when the wind turns. The bura comes off the mainland hard and without much warning, and the jugo builds swell along the whole open side of the island. Neither is dangerous if you are watching for it. Both are miserable if you are not.

In practice this means the caves are a plan A that sometimes becomes a plan B. A good skipper will look at the morning forecast and tell you honestly if the western coast is on today or if the day is going round the sheltered side instead. That is not a downgrade: the eastern half of the island has Telašćica, Lake Mir and Sali, and none of them care what the wind is doing.

Planning a cave day

The realistic shape of it is a speedboat day or a private charter with a skipper watching the forecast from the night before. Combine the caves with Sakarun and the Veli Rat lighthouse and you have the northern half of the island in one very full day. Add one of the navy tunnels and you have the strangest day out available from Zadar.

Bring a torch, bring something to keep a phone dry, and bring shoes you can climb wet limestone in. And if you get inside Strašna peć, remember that an emperor once paid for the staircase you walked up.
