The Zadar Nobody Photographs: Tunnels, Shelters and the Winter of 1943

Visitors walk Zadar's old town and assume they are looking at something continuous, a city that simply kept going for two thousand years. It did not. Most of what stands on this peninsula was put back after 1945, and the reason is one of the least-discussed episodes on this coast: between November 1943 and October 1944 Allied bombers destroyed around four fifths of Zadar, and the people who lived here spent that year underground.

Why Zadar at all

Between the wars this was Zara, an Italian enclave. The Treaty of Rapallo in 1920 handed the city and a narrow strip around it to Italy while leaving everything beyond that strip to the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, so Zadar spent two decades as an island that happened to be attached to the mainland: cut off by land, supplied by sea, and ringed with fortifications by a government that expected trouble from the hinterland.

When Italy left the war in September 1943 and German forces moved in, the city turned into a dot on Allied target maps. Reports reaching Allied command from Tito's partisans overstated the scale of the German presence here. The bombers came for a garrison that was, for the most part, not there.

The winter the city came down

The first heavy raid came on the evening of 2 November 1943. Another followed on 28 November, with more than two hundred dead. On 16 December around fifty American B-25 Mitchell bombers dropped roughly ninety tonnes of bombs, and the shelters at Cererija, in today's Voštarnica quarter, were hit: between 150 and 200 people died there in a single afternoon.

Eyewitness accounts of that day are hard reading. One shelter took a hit at the entrance and the rubble sealed it, and rescuers could hear the families inside while they worked at stone they could not move fast enough.

Allied records count thirty raids over the following year. Italian accounts written at the time claim fifty-four. Either way, much of the destruction came not from the explosions but from the fires afterwards, which is why photographs from 1945 show street after street of burnt-out shells rather than craters. Estimates of the dead range from under a thousand to four thousand; most historians settle near the lower figure, and only because so many people had already left.

They left in enormous numbers. Zadar had roughly 24,000 inhabitants when the war started. By the spring of 1944 fewer than 4,000 civilians remained in the city, and by the end of that year the count was around 6,000. Almost 80% of the buildings were destroyed and nearly all the rest were damaged.

The city had already dug in

None of this came as a surprise to the people who built Zadar's defences. From the late 1920s onward the Italian administration constructed a ring of bunkers, fortlets and defensive lines around the enclave, with concentrations behind Gaženice and in the Ploče and Crno areas. One of the larger works, an underground fort catalogued as C-47, was begun in the late 1930s and probably not finished until around August 1940.

The full extent of it has only recently been mapped. The military historian Jurica Vučetić has catalogued roughly 250 bunkers and tunnel structures across the wider Zadar area, and has said that satellite imagery did much of the work for him, because the concrete domes are obvious from above once you know what you are looking at. Some of the passages run about twenty metres below streets that now carry ice cream and rolling suitcases.

Renaissance walls, twentieth-century shelters

The most striking part of the network is not new construction at all. The Venetian bastions, built in the 16th century against the Ottomans, were hollowed out and converted into air raid shelters during the Italian administration. Research by David Štrmelj and Dejan Filipčić documents these adaptations of the Renaissance walls in detail, as one component of a much larger shelter-building programme run by the local authorities immediately before and during the Second World War.

So the same defensive works that are now inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage list as Venetian fortifications had a second military career four hundred years later, against a threat their builders could not have imagined. You walk over the result every time you go through the Land Gate.

What is left down there

Most of it is shut. The tunnels are damp, unstable and pitch black, several flood after heavy rain, and some entrances have been buried by later building work. In the Bokanjac district there are people who played in those tunnels as children and then sheltered in them again during the war of 1991 to 1995, which is a compact summary of the twentieth century in one neighbourhood.

An Interreg-funded project set out to survey and revive some of the sites. The ambition, doing what Pula managed with its Zerostrasse tunnels and Trieste with Kleines Berlin, has not been realised yet. For now the underground city stays a matter for historians, speleologists and the occasional guided walk.

The mirror image out on the islands

There is a strange counterpoint to all this an hour offshore. On Dugi Otok the Yugoslav navy cut tunnels into limestone cliffs to hide warships from aircraft; in Zadar people cut into the ground to hide from aircraft. Same instinct, same century, opposite ends of the same archipelago. The wrecks on the seabed between the two are the third part of the same story.

Why it belongs in a sailing guide

Because it explains what you are looking at. When you come back into the harbour at dusk and see the campaniles lined up above the walls, you are looking at a reconstruction of a place that lost four fifths of itself in a single year and was rebuilt without losing the Roman street grid underneath. Postwar planners kept the lines of the ancient city and filled the gaps with modern stone, which is why the old town reads as coherent from the water even though almost nothing in it is untouched.

The sunset sail gives you the whole peninsula in one frame from a few hundred metres offshore, which is the only place you can take it all in at once. Look at it knowing what happened here in the winter of 1943 and it stops being a pretty skyline and becomes something considerably more interesting.
