The Zadar Nobody Photographs: Tunnels, Shelters and the Winter of 1943

Visitors walk Zadar's old town and assume they are looking at something continuous, a place that simply survived. It is not. Most of what stands here was rebuilt, and the reason is one of the least-discussed episodes on this coast.

The winter the city came down

Zadar spent the interwar decades under Italian administration, and by late 1943, with Italy out of the war and the Germans in control, it became a target. The first heavy Allied raid came on the evening of 2 November 1943. Another followed on 28 November, with more than two hundred dead. On 16 December, around fifty American B-25 Mitchell bombers dropped roughly ninety tonnes of bombs, and the shelters at Cererija, today's Voštarnica quarter, were hit; between 150 and 200 people died there.

Eyewitness accounts of that day are hard reading. One shelter took a hit at the entrance, and the rubble sealed it: rescuers could hear families inside and could not get the stone off in time.

By the end of the campaign, around 80% of the buildings in Zadar were destroyed and almost all the rest damaged.

What is left underground

The city that was bombed had dug in. Shelters and tunnels were cut under and around Zadar in the years before and during the raids, and much of that network is still there, unmarked and mostly unvisited, under streets that now carry ice cream and rolling suitcases. Some passages are sealed, some serve as storage, and a few have been opened to the public over the years.

It is a strange counterpoint to the tunnels out on the islands. On Dugi Otok the navy cut tunnels into rock to hide warships from the air; in Zadar people cut them into the ground to hide from what came from the air. Same instinct, same decade, opposite ends of the same archipelago.

Why it belongs in a sailing guide

Because it explains what you see. When you sail back into the harbour at dusk and look at the old town, you are looking at a reconstruction of a place that lost four fifths of itself in a single winter, and rebuilt without losing the Roman street grid underneath. That is more impressive than a postcard, and you can see the whole of it from the water on a sunset sail.
