The Islands of the Zadar Archipelago: a Sailor’s Field Guide

Stand on Zadar's waterfront and the horizon is never empty: a wall of islands runs parallel to the coast as far as you can see in both directions. The Zadar archipelago counts over 300 islands, islets and reefs spread across roughly 400 square kilometres, which makes this the most broken-up piece of coastline in the Adriatic. Only about twenty of them have permanent residents. The rest are stone, sheep, olives and the occasional chapel.

No two of them have the same story. A Byzantine emperor wrote about one; another sold pottery across an empire; a third built its villages away from the sea to hide from pirates; a fourth has banned cars since before anyone thought that was fashionable. This is the field guide our skippers give guests who ask what, exactly, they are looking at.

Ugljan: the olive island

The closest island to the city, twenty minutes across the channel, and the one our day sailing trip knows best. Romans ran a serious olive oil operation at Muline on the northern tip two thousand years ago, pressing an estimated 85,000 litres a season of the oil they sold as Liburnian; the full story, including the Roman recipe for counterfeiting it, is in our guide to the hidden bays near Zadar.

The same slopes still grow olives today. Above Preko, the ruined fortress of St Michael sits at 265 metres with the best overview of the archipelago anywhere, and Kali on the eastern shore is still a genuine fishing town rather than a picture of one. Full details in the Ugljan guide.

Dugi Otok: the long one

Forty kilometres of ridge holding the archipelago's greatest hits: Sakarun beach, the cliffs and salt lake of Telašćica, the sea caves along the western shore, and the navy tunnels cut into the hillside in the middle of it.

The island entered history in the mid-10th century, when the Byzantine emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus recorded it in De administrando imperio under the name Pizuh. The western coast faces the open Adriatic and takes the weather; the eastern is a string of sheltered villages, Sali and Božava among them. Emperors change; the island keeps the better view. The whole thing is covered in the Dugi Otok guide.

Pašman: monasteries and the cleanest channel

South of Ugljan and joined to it by a bridge, Pašman moves at a monastic pace, literally: Benedictines have kept a monastery on Ćokovac hill since the 12th century, and it is still an active community. The channel between Pašman and the mainland changes current direction roughly every six hours, which locals credit for some of the clearest water on the coast. More in the Pašman guide.

Iž: the potters' island

Between Ugljan and Dugi Otok sits Iž, the only Adriatic island famous for pottery. The first written record of Iž ceramics dates to 1530, and for centuries its wood-fired pots travelled to markets across the Adriatic, along the coast and deep inland. The potters were called lopižari, after the lopiž, the round earthenware pot used for cooking, and at the height of the trade around seventy families on the island made a living from clay.

A handful of potters keep the craft alive today, and there is a collection in Veli Iž that holds the history together. The island has two villages, Veli Iž and Mali Iž, and a coastline of small bays that are usually empty. See the Iž potters guide.

Molat: three villages hiding from pirates

Inhabited since the Stone Age and first recorded in the 10th century, Molat holds three settlements, Molat, Zapuntel and Brgulje, all built a careful distance inland. The reason was practical: pirates. The sea facing the open Adriatic was dangerous for centuries, so the islanders farmed, kept sheep and kept their houses out of sight of passing sails. The harbours came later, once it was safe to admit you lived there. Details in the Molat guide.

Silba: no cars, and a tower built for a broken heart

Silba sits further out, shaped like a figure eight with the village at the waist. It has been car-free for decades: no cars, no motorbikes, just tractors allowed out for a few hours a day to move goods, and the postman's scooter. The official population is under three hundred and drops to a hundred and fifty or so in winter, then multiplies many times over in August.

In the middle of the village stands the Toreta, a 15-metre stone tower with a spiral staircase, built in the 19th century by a sea captain. The story attached to it is that he built it for a woman who did not wait for him. Whether or not that is true, it is the only thing on the island tall enough to see over the pines.

Rivanj, Sestrunj, Rava: the quiet ones

Between Ugljan and Dugi Otok runs a chain of islands most visitors never learn to name. Rivanj has 31 residents, 4.4 square kilometres and a high point called Lukočina at 112 metres. Sestrunj, fifteen square kilometres of macchia and olive terraces, has 48 residents and the 15th meridian running through the village, marked and worth the photograph, which we cover in Sestrunj and the 15th meridian. Rava, squeezed between Iž and Dugi Otok, counts a hundred or so people across two harbours.

No cars to speak of, no crowds, and off Rivanj lie the Tri Sestrice islets, the first swim stop of our day route. These islands are what the word "untouched" was invented for, and they are covered together in Rava, Rivanj and the small islands.

Premuda and the outer rim

At the archipelago's outer edge, Premuda guards two legends. One is an underwater cave system that divers call the Cathedral, where light comes down through gaps in the roof into a chamber big enough to lose your bearings in. The other lies at 66 metres: the wreck of the Austro-Hungarian battleship Szent István, torpedoed off this island in 1918 and known as the Titanic of the Adriatic.

Premuda is nine kilometres of stone with a few dozen winter residents, and it feels like the end of something, which geographically it is. See the Premuda guide, and for the rest of what is down there, our survey of the wrecks of the Zadar archipelago.

How to meet them

No ferry timetable strings these islands together; a boat does. Ferries run out from Zadar like spokes on a wheel, all of them back to the city, which means island hopping by public transport mostly means going home in between. Day trips cover Ugljan and its neighbours, speedboats reach Dugi Otok and beyond, and the three-day route sleeps among them. Every option is listed on the boat tours page. Start anywhere; the archipelago takes care of the rest.
