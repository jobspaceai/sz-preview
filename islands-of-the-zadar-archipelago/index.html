The Islands of the Zadar Archipelago: a Sailor’s Field Guide

Stand on Zadar's waterfront and the horizon is never empty: a wall of islands runs parallel to the coast as far as you can see in both directions. The Zadar archipelago counts over 300 islands, islets and reefs, and no two of them have the same story. A Byzantine emperor wrote about one; another sold pottery across an empire; a third built its villages away from the sea to hide from pirates. This is the field guide our skippers give guests who ask what, exactly, they are looking at.

Ugljan: the olive island

The closest island to the city, and the one our day sailing trip knows best. Romans ran a serious olive oil operation at Muline two thousand years ago, pressing an estimated 85,000 litres a season; the full story is in our guide to the hidden bays near Zadar. The same slopes still grow olives today.

Dugi Otok: the long one

Forty kilometres of ridge holding the archipelago's greatest hits: Sakarun beach, the Telašćica cliffs and salt lake, and the village of Božava under its pinewood. The island entered history in the mid-10th century, when the Byzantine emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus recorded it in De administrando imperio under the name Pizuh. Emperors change; the island keeps the better view.

Pašman: monasteries and the cleanest channel

South of Ugljan and joined to it by a bridge, Pašman moves at a monastic pace, literally: Benedictines have kept a monastery on Ćokovac hill since the 12th century. The channel between Pašman and the mainland changes current every six hours, which locals credit for some of the clearest water on the coast.

Iž: the potters' island

Between Ugljan and Dugi Otok sits Iž, the only Adriatic island famous for pottery. The first written record of Iž ceramics dates to 1530, and for centuries its wood-fired pots travelled to markets across the Adriatic and deep inland. A handful of potters keep the craft alive today.

Molat: three villages hiding from pirates

Inhabited since the Stone Age and first recorded in the 10th century, Molat holds three settlements, Molat, Zapuntel and Brgulje, all built a careful distance inland. The reason was practical: pirates. The sea facing the open Adriatic was dangerous for centuries, so the islanders farmed, kept sheep and kept their houses out of sight of passing sails.

Rivanj, Sestrunj, Rava: the quiet ones

Between Ugljan and Dugi Otok runs a chain of islands most tourists never learn to name. Rivanj has 31 residents; Sestrunj, fifteen square kilometres of macchia and olive terraces, has 48; Rava, squeezed between Iž and Dugi Otok, counts a hundred or so. No cars to speak of, no crowds, and off Rivanj lie the Tri Sestrice islets, the first swim stop of our day route. These islands are what the word "untouched" was invented for.

Premuda and the outer rim

At the archipelago's outer edge, Premuda guards two legends: an underwater cave system divers call the Cathedral, and, at 66 metres down, the wreck of the Austro-Hungarian battleship Szent István, torpedoed in 1918 and known as the Titanic of the Adriatic. That story deserves its own article, and it will get one.

How to meet them

No ferry timetable strings these islands together; a boat does. Day trips cover Ugljan and its neighbours, speedboats reach Dugi Otok and beyond, and the three-day route sleeps among them. Start anywhere; the archipelago takes care of the rest.
