Ship Tunnels of the Adriatic: Three on Dugi Otok You Can Sail Into

There are ship tunnels along the entire length of the Adriatic and almost nobody comes looking for them. Seventeen can still be named, cut into the coast so that a warship could be driven inside and vanish from the air. Three of them are on Dugi Otok, about an hour from Zadar in a fast boat, and those three are open water: no gate, no ticket, no guide waiting at the entrance. You steer in and the daylight stops behind you.

Why a navy cut ship-sized holes into a coastline

Yugoslavia had a long coast, a narrow sea and a standing assumption that it would one day have to fight somebody considerably larger. Its answer was to make the fleet hard to find rather than hard to sink. A ship at a pier can be photographed and counted from a reconnaissance aircraft, and from the late 1960s from a satellite. A ship inside a mountain cannot be counted at all.

The fleet was worth the effort because it was small and expensive. Across its whole existence the Yugoslav navy operated sixteen submarines, from the Sutjeska and Heroj boats of the 1950s and 1960s to the Sava class and the small Una class built for divers and mine-laying. Add the missile boats and torpedo boats of the fast strike force and you have a few dozen hulls that mattered. Cutting a hole in limestone is cheap next to replacing any one of them.

The Croatian word for these places is potkop, roughly an undercut. The official definition is unromantic and useful: a military underground port with organised berths, shelter for vessels and crews, space for maintenance and training, and protection from both attack and weather. The most intensive building ran from the 1950s to the end of the 1970s.

What one is like inside

Cold first. The rock holds the same temperature all year, so on an August afternoon the entrance behaves like a door into a cellar. Anyone who has just been swimming regrets it immediately.

Then the sound. An outboard at idle in a stone chamber a hundred metres long makes a noise nobody expects, and conversation comes back a fraction late. Most crews go quiet without being asked.

The shape is consistent: a high vaulted profile, walls carrying the marks of the drilling, concrete quays down both sides at deck height with old car tyres bolted on as fenders. Some have a depth gauge painted on the back wall. Most have graffiti near the entrance and nothing deeper in, because spray cans need light. The water is green where the sun reaches and black beyond that.

The three on Dugi Otok: Zagračina, Paprenica and Bukašin

Dugi Otok has three, which is more than any other island in this archipelago and more than most islands anywhere on this coast. Zagračina is at the northern end, in the bay of the same name near Božava. Paprenica and Bukašin are further south, both in bays near the village of Dragove, close enough to each other that a boat can look into both in the same hour.

All three were built as shelters for surface vessels rather than submarines, which matters because half the maps online label them submarine pens. The guides on Vis make the same correction about theirs. What used these were missile boats, torpedo boats and patrol craft, which is why the berths are shallow and the entrances wide rather than deep.

Zagračina is the one most people end up seeing, because it is nearest a village with a harbour and can also be reached over land, on a rough track from Božava that is fine on foot and unpleasant in a hire car. It is also the most knocked about of the three. Doors, fittings and anything worth removing went long ago, and what is left is the chamber, the quays and the water.

The question everyone asks is whether you are allowed in. The honest answer is that they are still formally military underground ports on the books of the Ministry of Defence, that nobody is there, that nothing is fenced or signed, and that local boats have used them as swimming stops for thirty years. Nobody will stop you and nobody will help you either. There is no lighting, no depth marking and no phone signal at the back.

The rules skippers here apply without discussion are short. Go in slowly and on the engine, never under sail, because there is no room to deal with a gust. Do not go in with any swell running, because a wave behaves differently in a closed chamber. Keep a bow watch for floating debris, stay out of side passages, and stay off the quays, which are unmaintained concrete over unknown depths.

Given a calm morning the good version is simple. Idle in, cut the engine, drift the last thirty metres, swim if the day is hot enough to make cold water a feature rather than a problem, and bring a torch, because the interesting detail is at the back where the light gives up.

The rest of them: Vis, Lastovo, Brač, Ploče and Pelješac

Vis has the famous one. Jastog, the lobster, sits in Parja bay under the small headland of Škojić, was built in 1982, runs about 127 metres into the rock and stands roughly 18 metres high. It was made for missile gunboats, and along with the base at Stupišće it is what the island now sells as a tour. If you want a tunnel with a guide and a printed ticket, Vis is where that exists.

Lastovo has three. Two are driven into the hill of Hum and run to about a hundred metres between them, built for a command post rather than for ships. The third, in Kremena bay, was a berth for submarines and torpedo boats and is the one that fits the pattern of the others.

Brač has three more, in the bays of Smrka, Maslinova and Kruščica near Milna. Smrka was closed territory for decades because of what was parked in it, and today the tunnels there mostly shelter the occasional fishing boat.

The largest concentration is not on an island at all. Six sit around Ploče and the north side of Pelješac: Raba, Komarni, Brijesta, Kremena, Duba and Soline. The Kremena one has a vertical shaft cut up through the rock as an escape route, which tells you what the people building it thought might happen. Several are now used by shellfish farmers, a reasonable second career for a large dry hole beside deep water.

The famous ones are in Montenegro

If you have seen photographs of Adriatic submarine tunnels, they were probably taken in the Bay of Kotor. Three were cut there: Rose, Rakite and Špilice. Rose is around a hundred metres long and eight metres wide, took two submarines at once, and unlike almost all the others it has a road connection and a separate entrance for lorries, which makes it a proper underground base rather than a shelter. Rakite and Špilice, east of it, held gunboats and torpedo boats and stayed in use into the early 2000s.

All three are in Montenegro, several hundred kilometres south of here, and none of them is reachable from Zadar in a day. The three on Dugi Otok are.

What the tunnels are now

Mostly swimming pools, and that is not a joke at their expense. The water inside is clean, deep enough to jump into from the quay, several degrees colder than the bay outside and permanently in shade, which in the middle of a Dalmatian August is a combination people will travel for. Locals on Brač and around Ploče have used them this way for years and are not keen on the news getting out.

Divers use them because the floor is flat, the entrance stays visible behind you and there is usually something on the bottom worth a torch. Kayak groups paddle into the shallower ones, which is the quietest way to do it and the one that conveys the scale best: a kayak inside a chamber built for a missile boat makes the arithmetic obvious.

Nothing is charged for any of this and nothing is maintained. Croatia has seventeen of these places and a heritage budget already spoken for several times over.

Fitting a tunnel into a day out of Zadar

Zadar is the only city on this coast with three ship tunnels inside a comfortable day trip, which is a strange thing to say about a place known for its sunsets. The run north up Dugi Otok already takes in Sakarun, the Veli Rat lighthouse and Božava, and Zagračina sits on the same stretch of coast without adding much to the day.

Our speedboat trips can put a tunnel on a Dugi Otok route, and it pairs naturally with the sea caves on the western coast if the sea is settled enough to get round there: one hole cut by water, one cut by people, on the same island in the same afternoon. On a multi-day sail it makes a good detour between Telašćica in the south and the northern beaches. Zadar itself has the mirror image of the story in its Italian-era bunkers and tunnels, dug into the city to hide people from the air rather than ships.

Two things to bring. A torch, because a phone light reaches about as far as your own arm. And a jacket, seriously, because everybody laughs at that advice until the engine goes quiet inside and they hear how much room there is around them.
