The Tunnels of Dugi Otok: Where a Navy Hid Its Ships Inside a Mountain

In this article

From the water, most of the coast of Dugi Otok reads as ordinary: rock, pine, the occasional cove, a village every few kilometres. Then the cliff opens, and there is a rectangular hole in it, sized for something considerably bigger than a fishing boat. The sea runs straight in and keeps going. You can steer inside.

Three doors in the rock

Dugi Otok has three of them. Two, called Bukašin and Paprenica, sit in bays near the village of Dragove. The third, Zagračina, is near Božava at the northern end of the island. In Croatian they are called potkopi, which translates roughly as undercuts or workings, and the official description is blunt: an underground naval base with berths for vessels, shelter for crews, maintenance space and protection from both enemy attack and weather.

The dimensions are consistent across all three. Roughly seventy metres cut into the rock, up to twelve metres of clearance at the entrance, and about six metres of water inside. That is enough for a missile boat, a patrol vessel or a small submarine to go in, tie up, and vanish.

Built to disappear

They were built by Yugoslavia as shelters for warships and submarines, part of a defensive scheme covering Zadar and the surrounding coast. The logic was aerial and, later, satellite reconnaissance. A ship at a pier can be counted from the air. A ship inside a mountain cannot be counted at all, and the entrance from directly overhead is a dark rectangle in a cliff that could be almost anything.

They are not unique. The same thinking produced the far larger submarine tunnels on Vis and in the Bay of Kotor, along with hundreds of hardened installations across the former Yugoslavia. The country spent an enormous share of its budget on the assumption that it would eventually have to fight somebody bigger, from a coastline it could not defend in the open. The tunnels here are simply the ones you can reach on a day out from Zadar.

The military use ended with the country. What was left behind is three large holes in the rock with no doors, no signage and no owner interested in the matter, which is why local swimmers adopted them almost immediately.

What it is like inside

Cold, first of all. The temperature drops the moment you pass the entrance, and the difference between the sun outside and the air twenty metres in surprises people every single time.

Then the acoustics. An outboard at idle inside a seventy metre stone chamber sounds like a cathedral organ played badly, and conversation gets strange: voices arrive back at you a fraction late. Most people go quiet without being asked.

The walls carry tool marks from the cutting, and in places there are still rails, mounts and fittings. Light comes in behind you and falls off fast, so the far end is a wall of dark that your eyes need a minute to accept. The water is clear and green near the mouth and simply black further in. Nothing about it is dressed up. There are no interpretation panels, no gift shop, no queue, and nobody collecting anything at the entrance.

What to actually do there

Go in slowly and let the engine idle, or better, cut it and drift the last part. Swim if the day is warm, because the temperature contrast in the water is part of the point. Bring a torch, because the useful detail is at the back where the light does not reach.

Do not go into side passages, do not touch anything that looks structural, and do not treat the far end as somewhere to explore on foot. These are unmaintained military structures with unknown debris on the bottom, and the interesting part is the space itself rather than anything you will find in it.

Part of a much longer habit

Hiding things in rock is not a Cold War invention on this coast. The Venetians put the fortress of St Michael on the ridge of Ugljan to watch the same channels. Zadar itself has an entire underground layer from the Second World War, when the city was bombed almost flat. The tunnels on Dugi Otok are the twentieth century version of a very old local reflex: when the sky becomes dangerous, go into the limestone.

How to see them

By boat, and preferably one with a skipper who knows which entrances are worth entering. Depths and debris vary between the three, some are silted at the mouth, and the entrances face the weather in ways that matter on the wrong day.

Our speedboat trips can include a tunnel on a Dugi Otok route. On a multi-day sail it makes a good detour between Telašćica in the south and Sakarun and the northern beaches, and it pairs well with the caves on the western coast if the sea is settled enough to get round there.

Bring a light. Bring a jacket, seriously. And when the engine goes quiet inside, listen for a moment, because the silence in there was built on purpose.
