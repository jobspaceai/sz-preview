Szent István: the Battleship That Sank on Camera

In the small hours of 10 June 1918, two Italian motor torpedo boats slipped between the escorts of an Austro-Hungarian squadron heading south through the Adriatic. One of them, MAS 15, commanded by Luigi Rizzo, put two torpedoes into the starboard side of the battleship Szent István at around a quarter past three in the morning.

She stayed up for nearly three hours. At 6:12 she rolled over and sank, off the island of Premuda, taking 89 men with her. Somebody was filming.

The ship

Szent István was the fourth and last of the Tegetthoff class, the only modern dreadnoughts Austria-Hungary ever built, and the only one of the four built in Hungary. Her keel was laid at the Ganz-Danubius yard in Fiume, today Rijeka, on 29 January 1912. She was launched on 17 January 1914 and commissioned on 13 December 1915.

The numbers: 152.18 metres long, 28 metres in the beam, around 20,000 tonnes, a crew of 1,094. Her main armament was twelve 30.5 centimetre Škoda guns in four triple turrets, which at the time she was designed was an unusual and ambitious arrangement.

She spent almost her entire career at anchor. The Austro-Hungarian battle fleet was a fleet in being: it existed to make the Allies keep ships in the Adriatic, and it very rarely went anywhere. When Szent István finally sailed on an offensive operation in June 1918, against the Otranto Barrage, it was one of the first serious actions of her life. She did not survive the transit.

The night of 9 to 10 June

The plan belonged to Miklós Horthy, then the fleet commander. Two battleship divisions were to move south under cover of darkness and hit the Allied barrage across the Strait of Otranto. Surprise was the entire concept.

Rizzo's two small motor boats were out on a routine patrol and stumbled into the squadron in the dark. That is the part that never stops being remarkable: a boat of a few dozen tonnes, powered by petrol engines, against a capital ship of twenty thousand. He fired, hit twice, and got away.

The torpedoes flooded two boiler rooms. Counter-flooding did not hold the list, the pumps could not keep ahead of the water, and an attempt to tow her into shallow water off Molat failed. Horthy cancelled the operation. The Otranto raid never happened, and the Austro-Hungarian fleet never sailed offensively again.

Filmed as she went

What makes Szent István extraordinary is not the sinking but the record of it. A cameraman aboard her sister ship Tegetthoff kept filming as the battleship heeled further and further over, until she turned turtle and went under with men sliding down her hull and jumping clear.

She is the only battleship of the First World War whose sinking was captured on film. That footage has been shown in practically every documentary about the naval war since, which is why a ship that fired her guns in anger almost never is one of the best known warships of her era. The wreck picked up the nickname the Titanic of the Adriatic, which is journalism rather than history, but it stuck.

Where she lies

The wreck rests upside down at 66 metres, a few kilometres off Premuda, in open water on the outer edge of the archipelago. She was located in the mid-1970s.

She is protected as a cultural heritage site by the Croatian Ministry of Culture. Diving her requires permits and technical certification, and the dive itself is deep, dark and cold, with an inverted hull, decompression obligations and no room for improvisation. This is not a site you talk your way onto. It is also, correctly, treated as a war grave.

Premuda itself

You do not have to dive her to visit. Premuda is nine kilometres of limestone with one village, white pebble beaches and almost nobody on it, and it is one of the more rewarding destinations at the outer edge of the archipelago.

The island's other underwater attraction is the Katedrala, a connected system of sea caves and crevices in the rock on the seaward side, running roughly eighty to a hundred metres with depths between ten and thirty. Light comes through the porous ceiling in shafts and there is no section that loses contact with daylight, which makes it an unusually safe cave dive and one that beginners can do with a guide. The smaller side tunnels around it are a different proposition and are for experienced divers only.

Getting out there

Premuda is a run rather than a hop: a speedboat day if the sea is settled, or a leg of a multi-day charter that also takes in Molat and the outer islands. It needs a decent forecast, because this is the exposed edge of the archipelago and there is nothing between it and Italy.

Anchor above the site on a calm day, look down at sixty six metres of blue, and remember that a battleship is directly beneath you, upside down, since a Monday morning in 1918. Some sightseeing does not need a viewing platform. If you want the wrecks you can actually see, the other three in this archipelago are all in snorkelling or recreational diving range.
