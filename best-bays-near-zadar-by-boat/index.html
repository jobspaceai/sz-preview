The Bays Near Zadar You Can Only Reach by Boat (One Comes With a Roman Factory)

Ask about beaches near Zadar and you will be sent to the city ones: fine for an afternoon, full by ten. The better answer lies across the channel. The islands facing the city, Ugljan, Rivanj and Sestrunj, are folded into dozens of coves that no bus can reach, and most of them have no name on the map apps.

These are the stops our skippers actually steer for on the day sailing route. None of them is a secret in the sense that nobody knows about them; they are simply on the wrong side of the water for anyone without a boat. Each has its own story, and one of them has two thousand years of it.

Tri Sestrice: the aquarium

The Three Sisters are a scatter of islets off Rivanj and the first stop of our full-day route. The channels between them are the closest thing this part of the Adriatic has to an aquarium: shallow, protected, and so clear you stop trusting your depth perception. People routinely think they are in two metres of water when they are in six.

We anchor in the lee, the SUP goes in the water, and this is reliably the stop where the schedule begins to slip. It never survives Tri Sestrice, and it is not supposed to. The bottom here is a mix of sand and seagrass, which is why the visibility is what it is, and the islets themselves are low enough that the maestral goes straight over the top of them.

Muline: lunch beside a Roman factory

Our lunch anchorage sits off Muline, a quiet hamlet on the northern tip of Ugljan, and here the story goes deep. In the first century AD this little bay was one of the Mediterranean's serious olive oil producers. Archaeologists have uncovered a Roman villa rustica with five oil presses that turned out an estimated 85,000 litres a season, shipped across the empire in some 3,240 amphorae of 26 litres each. It is the largest known Roman olive oil operation in Croatia.

The oil had a brand name. It was sold as Liburnian oil, after the people of this coast, and it was famous enough that the Roman cookbook attributed to Apicius includes a recipe for faking it: take Spanish oil, add helenium, cypress root, bay leaves and salt, and pass it off as Liburnian. There is no better proof of a premium product than a documented method for counterfeiting it.

Around the mill lie the remains of a late antique mausoleum and a three-nave early Christian basilica from the 5th century, its stone outlines still standing among the olives and cypresses. A faithful replica of the ancient mill was built beside the site in 2007, so you can see how the press actually worked rather than guessing from foundations. Two thousand years later the same slopes still grow olives, and you eat your lunch at anchor a stone's throw from where Rome bottled its finest.

Frnaža: the last long swim

On the northeast coast of Ugljan, Frnaža is the cove we save for the end of the day: pebbles, pines, and water clear to the anchor chain. On a weekday morning there is a fair chance you will share it with nobody at all. It is twenty minutes from the city at sailing speed, and it feels like another decade.

The reason it works as a last stop is the light. By late afternoon the sun is behind the ridge of Ugljan, the bay goes into shade, and the water turns a deep green that photographs badly and swims beautifully. Nobody is in a hurry to get back on board.

Ćinta and the surprise stop

Ćinta, with its shallow sandy bottom that goes bath-warm by afternoon, is often our surprise stop, the one the skipper adds between Tri Sestrice and Muline when wind and sea allow. Every crew gets a different one, and none of them are printed in any brochure.

That flexibility is not a marketing line, it is how small boats work. If the bura is up, the whole route shifts to the sheltered side of the island and we find bays that are useless in a maestral and perfect that day. If it is flat calm we go further out. The wind decides the itinerary, and the wind is the only member of the crew that never negotiates.

Ošljak and Galevac: two islets worth a detour

Halfway across the Zadar channel sits Ošljak, the smallest inhabited island in the Adriatic: 0.39 square kilometres, about 35 permanent residents, a single settlement and a hill covered in cypresses. It has a bleak backstory. In the 17th century it served as the lazaret for the whole Zadar region, the quarantine island where plague and leprosy cases were isolated, and locals still sometimes call it Lazaret. Today it is a handful of houses, a small harbour and no cars, for the excellent reason that there is nowhere to drive.

Just off Preko, on the Ugljan shore opposite, lies Galevac, known locally as Školjić, a pine-covered islet a two-minute row from the village with a Franciscan monastery on it dating to the 15th century. It is one of those places that seems too small to hold what it holds. You can swim to it from Preko, and people do.

What is above the bays

If you look up from the anchorage at Preko you will see a ruin on the ridge. That is the fortress of St Michael, at 265 metres the highest point of Ugljan, with a view that takes in the whole archipelago in one turn of the head. It is a walk rather than a swim, but it explains the geography better than any map, and we cover it in the guide to Fort St Michael. The island itself, villages and olive oil included, is in the Ugljan guide.

How to reach them

Tri Sestrice, Muline and Frnaža are fixed points of our day sailing trip from Zadar; the surprise stop belongs to the wind. If you have half a day rather than a full one, the half-day sail covers the nearest of them. On a private charter, the whole route bends to your wishes.

Bring what you would bring to a beach with no shop attached, which is more or less the list we give every guest. And if these bays give you the taste for it, the same logic scales up: the same coast, further out, is the rest of the archipelago. A boat does not follow a road, and in these islands, that is the entire idea.
