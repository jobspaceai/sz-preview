Sestrunj: the Island on the 15th Meridian Where Compasses Misbehaved

Somewhere on Sestrunj there is a bench. It is a mosaic bench, made and given to the island by the artist Boška Kliman, and if you sit on it you are sitting on the 15th meridian east: the line of longitude that defines Central European Time. When your phone says it is three o'clock in Zagreb, Vienna, Rome, Madrid or Zadar, this is the line that hour is measured from.

What the 15th meridian actually is

The Earth turns 360 degrees in 24 hours, which works out at 15 degrees an hour. Time zones were built on that arithmetic: every zone takes a meridian that is a multiple of 15 degrees as its reference. Greenwich sits at zero. One hour east of it, at 15 degrees east, is the reference line for Central European Time, which is what nine countries and roughly 200 million people set their clocks by.

That line crosses a lot of Europe and very little of it is memorable. It passes through Germany, the Czech Republic, Austria, Slovenia and Croatia, and out here it runs through the Adriatic across a handful of islands. Sestrunj is one of them, and it has done the sensible thing and put a bench on it.

The compasses that would not settle

Long before anyone put a bench on a line, sailors knew there was something odd about these waters. Local accounts hold that magnetic variations near the island made ships' compasses unreliable, so navigators passing Sestrunj could not entirely trust their instruments. Treat that as island tradition rather than surveyed fact, because we have not found a modern survey confirming it. But it is repeated consistently enough, and by enough people who fish here, that it is worth knowing.

It makes for a strange overlap: an island that quietly defines what time it is for half of Europe, and that reputedly once made it hard to know which way you were going.

Forty-eight people, three harbours, no cafe

Sestrunj covers fifteen square kilometres between Ugljan, Rivanj and Dugi Otok. Around 48 people live there. Forest and macchia cover most of the surface, and what is not forest is olive terrace and dry stone wall. The village sits on the hilltop rather than the shore, in the position it has held since the days when being visible from the sea was a liability, and it has kept its shape.

There are three harbours, Hrvatinj, Kablin and Triluke, and there is no shop and no cafe on the island. That last detail is the one to plan around. If you anchor here you are self-sufficient: bring water, bring lunch, and do not expect to buy a coffee ashore. What you get in exchange is an island that has almost no traffic of any kind, on foot or on the water.

The wreck offshore

Off Sestrunj lies the Ledenik, a refrigerated cargo ship that sank in 2008 and now sits shallow enough that you can see her from the surface with a mask. She is the most accessible wreck in the archipelago and the only one on our list that does not require a tank, which makes her a genuine attraction rather than a diver's footnote. The rest of the archipelago's wrecks, most of them considerably deeper, are covered in our guide to the shipwrecks of the Zadar archipelago.

Tri Sestrice, just north

The islets called Tri Sestrice, the Three Sisters, lie in the water off the northern end of this stretch and mark the first swim stop of our full-day route. The channels between them are shallow, protected and clear enough that you can count the stones on the bottom from the deck, and most of our guests spend an hour there without realising they are within sight of the meridian. They belong properly to the story of Rivanj and Rava, the two smallest inhabited islands in this part of the archipelago, which sit just across the channel.

What Sestrunj is actually like ashore

The walk up from any of the three harbours takes twenty to thirty minutes and climbs through olive terraces that are still worked, mostly by people who come over from Zadar for the season. The village is a cluster of stone houses on the ridge with a church and a graveyard, and in the middle of a weekday in June you can walk through it without meeting anyone. Forest covers most of what is not terraced, which is unusual out here: the outer islands are typically bare, and Sestrunj is green.

There is no organised anything. No signposted trails, no interpretation boards, no ticket office at the meridian. You find the bench by asking, or by walking until you see it. That is a feature rather than an oversight, and it is the reason the island is worth a morning if you are the sort of person who reads a page like this one to the end.

Getting there

There is a ferry, infrequent, and there is a boat, which is how anyone sensible arrives. On a day sailing trip we pass along its coast; on a private charter you can anchor and go looking for the bench. It is not signposted the way a monument would be, and there is nobody to ask on the way up. On an island of 48 people, that feels about right.
