Sestrunj: the Island on the 15th Meridian Where Compasses Misbehaved

Somewhere on Sestrunj there is a bench. It is a modest, artistic sort of bench, and if you sit on it you are sitting on the 15th meridian east: the line of longitude that defines Central European Time. When your phone says it is three o'clock in Zagreb, Vienna, Rome or Zadar, this bench is where that hour is measured from.

The compasses that would not settle

Long before anyone put a bench on a line, sailors knew there was something odd about these waters. Local accounts hold that magnetic variations near the island made ships' compasses unreliable, so navigators passing Sestrunj could not entirely trust their instruments. In an era when a compass was the difference between arriving and not arriving, that made this small island a place to be careful.

It is a strange overlap: an island that quietly defines what time it is for half of Europe, and that once made it hard to know which way you were going.

Forty-eight people and three harbours

Sestrunj covers fifteen square kilometres of macchia, olive terraces and dry stone walls, and 48 people live there. It has three harbours, Hrvatinj, Kablin and Triluke, and no crowds in any of them. Off its shore lies the wreck of the Ledenik, a refrigerated cargo ship that sank in 2008 and now sits shallow enough to snorkel.

Just north, the islets called Tri Sestrice, the Three Sisters, mark the first swim stop of our full-day route, so most of our guests pass within sight of the meridian without knowing it. Now you know it.

Getting there

There is a ferry, infrequent, and there is a boat, which is how anyone sensible arrives. On a day sailing trip we pass along its coast; on a private charter you can anchor and go looking for the bench. It is not signposted the way a monument would be. That, on an island of 48 people, feels about right.
