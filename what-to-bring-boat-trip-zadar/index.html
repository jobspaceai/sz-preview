What to Bring on a Day Boat Trip in Zadar (From People Who Watch You Forget)

We have run enough day trips out of Zadar to predict, with unsettling accuracy, what a group will be missing by two in the afternoon. It is almost never the interesting stuff. It is water, a shirt with sleeves, and shoes. Here is the whole list, in order of how much it actually matters, written by people who spend the summer watching guests discover what they forgot.

Water: at least one litre per person

There is drinking water aboard and it is included. It is still not enough. Eight hours in July, on a boat with no shade except what the mainsail throws, with swimming in between and a breeze evaporating sweat off you before you notice it, dehydrates people faster than they expect. Bring a litre each and you will drink all of it.

Freeze half a bottle the night before and top it up in the morning. You get cold water until early afternoon, which is exactly when you want it.

Sun protection: not optional

A hat that will not blow off, high-factor sunscreen, and something with sleeves. Three separate mistakes get made here every week.

The first is underestimating reflection. The sea throws light back up at you, so you burn from below as well as above: under the chin, behind the knees, the tops of the feet. The second is the breeze, which hides how hot you are getting. On a sailing boat moving at six knots, the apparent wind makes 33 degrees feel comfortable while the sun does exactly the same damage it would on a beach. The third is reapplication. Salt water and towels take sunscreen off, and one application at nine in the morning is worth very little by one in the afternoon.

A long-sleeved shirt for the middle of the day is the single best decision most guests do not make. Linen, cotton, a rash vest, anything. Wear it wet if you like.

A layer against the wind

This one surprises somebody every single season. Thirty-five degrees on the quay in the morning can feel like eighteen on the return leg with the maestral behind you and wet swimwear on. The maestral is a thermal wind that fills in during the afternoon, which means the strongest breeze of the day tends to arrive exactly when everyone has been in and out of the water four times.

A light windproof jacket weighs nothing, packs into a fist, and rescues the last two hours of the day. If you only take one piece of advice off this page, take this one, because it is the item nobody thinks to bring to Croatia in August.

Shoes, and why limestone wins

The islands here are limestone, and where it meets the sea it is sharp, uneven and covered in things that puncture skin. Reef shoes or any cheap pair of water shoes solve the whole problem for about ten euros. Bring them if you plan to walk ashore anywhere, which on most of our routes you will.

Sea urchins are the other reason. They sit in rock crevices in shallow water, they are easy to miss, and the spines break off under the skin. Nobody has ever regretted the shoes.

Something extra to eat

On the full-day trip lunch is included, but it is a light meal, usually a pasta salad with meat. It is designed to be eaten at anchor without a galley crew, not to be a restaurant course. If you are a big eater, travelling with hungry teenagers, or particular about food, bring a supplement. Fruit, nuts, a sandwich, anything that survives a bag in the sun.

On the half-day trip there is no meal at all. Water and beer are aboard; everything else comes with you.

Tell us in the booking note about allergies or anything you cannot eat. We would rather know a week early than at the anchorage with two hours of sailing between you and a shop.

If you are bringing children

We carry child-size life jackets, but tell us the ages when you book so the right sizes are actually aboard. Beyond that: a change of dry clothes, more snacks than you think, and something to do during the passages, because the sailing between swim stops is the part small children find least interesting. The swimming they will handle by themselves and then some.

Seasickness, honestly

Most days in this archipelago are flat, because the islands break the swell before it reaches the channels. That is the whole geographic advantage of sailing from Zadar rather than from an exposed coast. But if the jugo has been blowing, there is a sea running, and if you know you are susceptible, take the tablets before departure rather than after the first wave. They do very little once you already feel it.

The other things that help are free: stay on deck, look at the horizon or at land, keep something in your stomach, and stay out of the cabin.

The small stuff that earns its place

A towel, obviously, and a second one if you feel strongly about dry towels. A dry bag or a simple zip bag for phone, keys and documents, because water gets everywhere on a boat and the tender ride ashore is the classic moment for a phone to go in. Sunglasses with a strap. A power bank, since there are 12-volt sockets aboard but not enough for eight people. Cash in small notes for a konoba or a park fee. And a plastic bag for wet swimwear at the end of the day.

If you take photographs seriously, a lens cloth. Salt spray dries on glass within minutes and every shot after that has a haze on it.

What to leave behind

Hard suitcases, which have nowhere to go on a boat and slide around when she heels. Heels, for the same reason plus several others. Glass bottles, if you can avoid them. Drones, unless you have checked the rules, because parts of this coast including the Kornati and Telašćica are protected areas with their own restrictions.

And leave behind any expectation of mobile signal in the outer bays. That last one is not a warning. It is the point.

Before you leave the flat

We sail from Marina Tankerkomerc, Gat 2, in Zadar. Arrive fifteen minutes early rather than on the hour, because boarding eight people, stowing bags and running the safety briefing takes longer than anyone plans for, and the departure slot is the departure slot.

Check the month you are travelling in against our month-by-month guide, read the wind article so the skipper's decisions make sense on the day, and then stop planning. The rest of it is a bag with water, a shirt, shoes and a jacket, and eight hours where the only real decision is which bay to swim in next.
