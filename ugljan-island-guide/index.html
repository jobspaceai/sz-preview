Ugljan: the Garden of Zadar, and the Island That Sold Oil to Rome

Ugljan is the wall of green you see from Zadar's waterfront, close enough that the ferry takes twenty minutes and the lights of its villages are part of the city's night view. Zadar has called it the garden of the city for as long as anyone can remember, and the name is literal: a large share of the terraces, fields and olive groves on the island belong to families who live across the channel and come over to work them. It is also the island our day sailing trip knows best.

Two thousand years of olives

Olive growing here goes back to the Illyrians and was already industrial under Rome. The hills are terraced almost to the ridgeline with dry stone walls that nobody alive built, and the trees standing on them are frequently older than the houses below. Local estimates put the number of olive trees on the island in the hundreds of thousands, which for fifty square kilometres of limestone is an absurd density, and explains why Ugljan reads as solid green from the Zadar side while most of its neighbours read as grey.

Muline: a Roman factory in a quiet bay

On the island's northern tip lies Muline, today a hamlet with a few houses and a jetty. In the first century AD it was one of the Adriatic's serious olive oil producers. Archaeologists have uncovered a Roman villa rustica with five presses and estimated seasonal output at around 85,000 litres, sold across the empire as Liburnian oil and shipped in roughly 3,240 amphorae of 26 litres each. Around the mill lie a late antique mausoleum and the stone outlines of a three-nave early Christian basilica from the 5th century, still visible among the olives and cypresses. A faithful replica of the ancient mill was built beside the site in 2007, which is the rare case of a reconstruction that actually helps you read the ruin.

We anchor off Muline for lunch on the full-day route. It is a good place to think about how little has changed: the same slopes still grow olives, and the oil still tastes like the reason somebody built a factory here.

The fort that was built to keep Zadar down

The highest point of the island, 265 metres above Preko, carries the fort of St Michael. Venice built it in 1203, right after the Fourth Crusade had been diverted to sack Zadar, and the purpose was not to defend the city but to watch it and to make sure it did not rebuild itself into a rival. For centuries afterwards the fort was the eye Venice kept on the channel.

The reason people climb it now has nothing to do with Venice. From the walls the entire archipelago lays itself out in order, island behind island, all the way to the Kornati on a clear day. It is the single best overview of the water we sail, and it costs nothing but a forty-minute walk up from Preko. The fort itself is a ruin with a modern viewing terrace bolted into it, graffiti in the old window openings, and a bell that somebody still rings.

Kali: the fleet that still goes out

Kali, first mentioned in a document from 1283, is not a resort and has never tried to be. It is a fishing village with one of the largest fleets in Croatia, somewhere around seventy purse seiners, and generations of families who have worked sardines and anchovies as a business rather than a heritage exercise. Kali crews have fished the Atlantic, the Pacific and the coasts of two other continents, and the village funded itself on the proceeds. If you tie up here in the evening you will hear engines and winches rather than a DJ.

Seven villages and a commute

The island holds around 6,000 people across seven villages: Ugljan, Lukoran, Sutomišćica, Poljana, Preko, Kali and Kukljica, strung along the sheltered eastern shore in that order from north to south. Preko is the largest and the ferry port, twenty minutes from Zadar, and enough of its residents work in the city that the morning boat functions as a commuter service rather than a tourist attraction. That is the thing that separates Ugljan from every other island on this list: it never emptied out. Where Molat went from a thousand residents to under two hundred, Ugljan stayed populated, because the city was always close enough to work in and come home from.

At the southern end a short bridge at Ždrelac joins it to Pašman, so the two are effectively one 40-kilometre ridge with a gap in the middle. The bridge has 16.5 metres of clearance and a current that can run to five knots underneath it, which is a detail that matters if you are sailing rather than driving.

The bays

Ugljan's coast is folded into dozens of coves, and the good ones have no road. Frnaža, on the northeastern shore, is where we take the last long swim of the day: pebbles, pines, and water clear to the anchor chain. Ćinta has a shallow sandy bottom that goes bath-warm by afternoon. Off Preko lie the islets of Galevac, with its Franciscan monastery under a wood, and Ošljak, the smallest inhabited island in the Adriatic, both regular stops on the half-day trip. Why it works as a first sailing day

Because it is close. The Zadar channel is sheltered by the island itself, the crossing takes under an hour under sail, and if the wind dies you have lost nothing but the sail. Families with small children, guests unsure about seasickness, and anyone with an afternoon flight all end up here, and none of them feel short-changed: this is still the Adriatic that put the archipelago on postcards, only twenty minutes from a parking space in town. Sail south from here and you are on Pašman within the hour; sail west and you are into the open middle of the archipelago.
