Sakarun Beach: Croatia’s White-Sand Bay, and the Lighthouse Built on Egg Yolks

Every summer the photos of Sakarun do the rounds: a crescent of white sand on Dugi Otok, water in three shades of turquoise, pines down to the shore. The photos are accurate. The crescent is around 800 metres long, the shallows run out for a good 350 metres over pale sand, and the pinewood behind the beach is old Aleppo pine that throws real shade at midday. It is the closest thing the Adriatic has to a Caribbean lagoon, and small children can wade further out than their parents can throw a ball.

The beach sits on the northwestern coast of Dugi Otok, facing the open sea, in a bay called Saharun on some charts and Sakarun on others. Both spellings are in use locally and neither is wrong.

Why the water is that colour

There is no filter and no mystery. The seabed here is fine pale sand ground down from limestone over thousands of years, and it continues out under the water for hundreds of metres before the depth drops away. Light goes down, hits the white bottom, and comes back up through a shallow layer of very clean water. That is the entire recipe. The same effect is why the shallows read almost white close to shore, turquoise further out, and then switch abruptly to deep blue where the sand ends.

The sand itself is a finite thing, which is a point worth making. Sakarun is inside a protected area of some 438 hectares managed under the Croatian Natura 2000 network, and a study on the bay found that clearing away the banks of dried Posidonia seagrass that wash up in winter, done to make the beach look tidier for summer, was quietly taking beach sediment away with them. The seagrass banquettes hold the sand in place and absorb wave energy. The sandy part of the beach has been eroding. So if you arrive in June and find a metre-high wall of brown seagrass at the back of the beach, that is not neglect. That is the beach's own defence system.

Why the boat wins

What the photos leave out is the journey. By land, Sakarun means a ferry to Brbinj, a drive across the island and a walk down from the car park, in company with several hundred people making the same trip on the same timetable, because the ferry decides when everybody arrives and when everybody leaves.

By boat from Zadar it is a straight run across the channel. You anchor in the bay itself, swim to the sand from the stern, and when the midday crowd builds you simply move along to the next cove. There are several within a few minutes: Brbišćica under its cliff, the little inlets towards Veli Rat, and a string of unnamed nicks in the coast where the only footprint on the shore is yours. No car can follow you there, and no timetable applies.

The lighthouse and the hundred thousand yolks

Round the corner from Sakarun, at the island's northwestern tip, stands the Veli Rat lighthouse. Built in 1849 under Austro-Hungarian rule, it is, at 42 metres, the tallest lighthouse on the Adriatic. Its facade is a warm ochre yellow, and here the story gets good: according to local tradition, the builders mixed around one hundred thousand egg yolks into the plaster to make it withstand the salt wind.

Whether the recipe is fact or legend, the paint has outlasted empires, and the lighthouse still works, its light visible more than twenty miles out. It stands on a low sandy point with two small bays either side, so on a speedboat trip it makes the natural photo stop after the beach, and there is a good swim to be had off the rocks below it. The longer version of the story, keepers included, is in our guide to the Veli Rat lighthouse.

What else is within an hour

Dugi Otok is forty kilometres long and Sakarun is one item on a fairly deep list. South along the same western coast are the sea caves, including Golubinka, where an opening in the roof drops a single beam of light into a chamber you swim into. Further south the island ends in the cliffs and salt lake of Telašćica nature park. In the middle of the island, the Yugoslav navy cut tunnels into the hillside big enough to hide missile boats, and they are still there, open and empty.

On the eastern, sheltered side, the villages are quieter: Sali with its fishing harbour and its brass band, Božava under a pinewood at the northern end. All of it is one island, and none of it is more than an hour apart by boat.

When to go

June and September give you the bay at its best: warm sea, thin sand traffic, low light. In July and August, arrive before eleven or stay after four; the middle of the day belongs to the excursion boats, and although the bay is big enough to share, the early morning there is a different experience altogether. The bay faces northwest, so it is exposed to the maestral in the afternoon and can get a small chop; nothing dangerous, but if you want glass, go early. More on the seasons in the best time to sail from Zadar.

How to fit Sakarun into your trip

Honesty first: on a one-day sailing trip from Zadar, a sailboat does not reach Sakarun. The distance needs more time than an eight-hour day allows, and anyone who tells you otherwise is planning to motor hard and give you an hour on the beach. There are two good ways in.

Fast: our speedboat trips do Sakarun, the Veli Rat lighthouse and a lunch stop in one day, and you are back in Zadar for dinner. Slow: on the three-day sailing route, Sakarun is the closing chapter, the morning of day three, after the Kornati and a night at anchor in Telašćica. Arriving at that beach with the day's first light, before a single excursion boat has crossed the channel, is the kind of moment people book entire holidays around.
