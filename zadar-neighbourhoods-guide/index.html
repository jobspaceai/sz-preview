Zadar Neighbourhood by Neighbourhood: a Guide for People Who Arrived by Boat

Most guests see two pieces of Zadar: the marina their boat is in, and the old town they walk through on the first evening. The rest is bigger and odder than that, and its neighbourhoods are easy to read from the water, because Zadar is laid out as a peninsula, then a bay, then the next bay.

Thirty seven councils, and a peninsula almost nobody lives on

Zadar has no city districts. It runs on thirty seven local councils, and twelve are not in the city at all but out in the archipelago: Silba, Olib, Premuda, Ist, Molat, Iž and Rava all have one. Relja, Kolovare and Borik, meanwhile, are on everybody's lips and nobody's official list.

Poluotok, the peninsula, is a council and the strangest one: every monument in the city stands on that strip, and almost nobody lives there. Nobody has counted properly, because the city's own cultural strategy admits no demographic study was ever made, but the electoral roll fell from 4,441 voters in 2011 to 3,275 in 2019. Three subtractions did it: the raids of 1943 and 1944, the departure of almost the entire Italian population by 1947, and flats becoming holiday flats.

Eat around Kalelarga, and do not expect to swim except off the steps of the Sea Organ. From the marina it is a kilometre by barkajol, the rowed ferry across the harbour, half as much again on foot. Our guide to the old town covers what stands on it.

Arbanasi: the village Venice imported in 1726

Southeast of the old town is a quarter founded in one deliberate act, by people from another country who kept their language for three hundred years. The first surviving record is dated 23 March 1726: fifty six people led by two brothers of the Petani family, leaving the villages of Šestani, Briska and Livari on the far side of Lake Skadar, then Ottoman territory.

The man usually credited is Vicko Zmajević, born in Perast in 1670, archbishop of Bar from 1701 and then of Zadar until his death in 1745. Venice wanted the emptied hinterland repopulated, and its governor Nicolò Erizzo II signed the orders granting the newcomers land, which is why the place was called Borgo Erizzo. Zmajević paid for the church of Our Lady of Loreto himself, built between 1734 and 1737, and Arbanasi stayed a separate settlement until 1944.

The language is the real story. Arbanaški is an archaic Gheg dialect of Albanian, layered over three centuries with Venetian, Čakavian Croatian and Turkish words, and spoken nowhere else on earth. Fluent speakers are estimated at 150 to 300, mostly the grandparent generation, and it has been on Croatia's register of protected cultural heritage since 2010. You will not hear it in a café, but you will see it in the surnames.

Voštarnica and Relja: wax, maraschino and a Roman cemetery

Voštarnica starts immediately behind the marina, the first real neighbourhood most guests walk through without noticing. The name translates the Italian Ceraria: there was a wax and candle works here. From the late nineteenth century this shore was the city's industrial side, and its industry was maraschino, the cherry liqueur Zadar shipped to half of Europe. Drioli set up in 1768 and Luxardo in 1821, and in 1946 the survivors merged into Maraska, whose building still stands here.

It paid for that position. On 2 November 1943, in the first raid, a direct hit destroyed shelter number 16 here and killed more than 150 civilians in a few minutes. Nothing on the street tells you. Today it is bakeries, grills and konobas cheaper than anything on the peninsula, five minutes from your boat.

Inland behind the next bay is Relja, officially part of the Jazine I council and called Relja by everyone anyway, after the family that owned the land. This is the shopping half of Zadar, market and bus station and malls, and also its oldest cemetery: Roman Iader buried its dead here for five centuries, and archaeologists have lifted more than three thousand graves out of the ground.

Jazine and Kolovare: where the city gets into the water

Jazine is the bay southeast of the harbour, and has two councils because it is that kind of place. The harbour was rebuilt between 2013 and 2019, and its promenade fills with prams and joggers every evening. Walk it at seven and you see the city being a city.

Round the neck of the peninsula, half an hour from the marina, is Kolovare, the town beach: pebbles and concrete under pines, beach bars, and most of Zadar in swimming shorts from June onward. At the end of it is the open air pool of about 1950, with a fifty metre lane pool and a diving tower of three, five and ten metres, the only one in Croatia where you land in the sea.

Borik, Puntamika and Diklo: the holiday coast

Northwest of the old town the shore turns into the Zadar built for visitors. Borik, administratively part of Puntamika, is the hotel and campsite strip three and a half kilometres out, pines behind the beach. Its oldest hotel, the Barbara, went up in 1970 to a design by Zdravko Bregovac and Ivan Filipčić.

Puntamika is the headland beyond it, and it holds the best small building in this half of Zadar: the Oštri rat lighthouse of 1869, fourteen metres of stone with its spiral staircase climbing the outside of the tower rather than the inside. You pass it leaving the harbour on every trip we run.

Beyond that is Diklo, a fishing village long before it was a suburb. It appears in the will of the Zadar prior Andrija in 918 and only joined Zadar in 1985. Low houses, pebble coves, two serious fish restaurants, almost no visitors.

Bili Brig, Stanovi, Smiljevac and Višnjik: where Zadar actually lives

Behind all of it is the Zadar nobody photographs. Stanovi is older than it looks, a suburb begun in the early nineteenth century. Bili Brig filled with family houses from the late 1960s and with blocks in the 1980s, and with more than ten thousand residents it is the biggest neighbourhood in the city. Smiljevac was marshland and abandoned bunkers before the towers arrived. Crvene Kuće, the Red Houses, is named after a single one, a nineteenth century villa of reddish brick belonging to the mayor Nikola Trigari.

Višnjik is worth the twenty five minute walk. The Yugoslav army barracks there were cleared for the city sports complex, and the Krešimir Ćosić hall of 2008 seats 6,503, or around 9,000 for basketball, which is not a pastime here but the local religion. Vidikovac, the council on the high ground north of town, earns its name, which means lookout: the whole city, the Velebit ridge, the islands, and the restored underground fort C-47. Zadarski list reports that almost nobody goes, an odd fate for the best panorama in Zadar. That concrete has its own story in our piece on the Italian bunkers.

Reading it from the water

All of it makes sense from a few hundred metres offshore and nowhere else. Coming in from the northwest you pass the Puntamika lighthouse, then Borik, then the long low shore of the built city, then the peninsula standing out of the sea with its bell towers, then the gap into the harbour with Voštarnica on one side and the walls on the other. Behind the line, the dome at Višnjik and the Velebit ridge.

That is the city's history in the right order, left to right, and it takes twenty minutes to motor past. Our evening sail gives you the whole thing with the light behind it, and the day trip gives it to you twice.
