Zadar Beaches: The Ones You Walk To, and the Ones You Sail To

Ask ten people about Zadar beaches and you get the same four names: Kolovare, Borik, Puntamika, Diklo. All four are good, and in the second week of August all four are full by half past nine in the morning. That is arithmetic rather than a complaint: seventy thousand residents plus their summer guests, against a few kilometres of coast. The other half of the answer is across the channel, and appears on no bus map.

Kolovare, the beach everyone means

Kolovare starts a ten-minute walk southeast of the Land Gate and runs the length of its own street. It is two things at once: bands of fine pebble, and long concrete platforms with ladders into the water. Old pines come almost to the waterline, which matters more than the pebbles do: between noon and four the scarce resource here is shade, not space.

Behind the beach are bars, a few restaurants and a pool with a diving board. The city treats Kolovare as one beach but samples its water at two separate points, Kolovare-hotel and Kolovare-Mulić, which tells you how much shoreline is involved. In 2020 the sea wall, paving and lighting were rebuilt in a project worth roughly 2.2 million kuna.

Locals will tell you the shore was built out after the Second World War with rubble from the bombed city, and since Zadar lost around four fifths of its buildings in that war, material was not the problem. It is the only serious beach you can walk to from the old town.

Borik, Puntamika, Diklo and the rest of the city coast

Go north and Zadar turns into its holiday version. Borik, about three kilometres out, is the hotel and campsite zone: pine wood, coarse sand mixed with pebble, water shallow far enough out for small children, playgrounds and rental stands. The old Camp Borik is now a five-star site under the Falkensteiner name, which changed the prices and not the pines. Buses 5, 5A and 8 run out here, and in July the 8 is the fullest bus in Dalmatia.

Puntamika sits on the small peninsula just beyond, roughly three and a half kilometres from the centre: quieter, more broken up, small pebbles and rock shelves and concrete terraces rather than one strand. At its point stands the Oštri Rat lighthouse, built of stone in 1869, the only lighthouse in the Zadar area standing on the mainland instead of an island. Diklo, a village the same distance northwest, is not a beach in the singular at all: a run of small pebble coves and concrete platforms along the whole waterfront, a few konobas, almost no tourist infrastructure. It is where Zadar swims on a Tuesday evening in June.

Three more. Vitrenjak, also called Uskok, sits between two marinas, is named for the wind that gets up there in the afternoon, and is the best-adapted beach in the city for wheelchair users, with two ramps into the water. Punta Bajlo, below Arbanasi, is free and shaded and was rebuilt in 2019. Jazine, the bay that cuts the peninsula off from the rest of the city, is a promenade rather than a swimming spot.

Forty minutes, and the crowd is gone

Now the part everyone who keeps a boat here knows and nobody puts on a poster. From Marina Zadar to a decent anchorage on the far side of the channel is about forty minutes when the maestral is doing its job. At the end of those forty minutes there is a bay with pines to the waterline, six metres of water that reads like two, and a fair chance of nobody else in it.

These places are not secret. Most are named on the chart and have a village a kilometre inland. They are empty because there is no road down to the water, no parking and no bus, so the only people who arrive are the ones who arrived by sea. The city coast is short, straight and served by buses; the island coast facing it is folded into dozens of coves and served by nothing.

Tri Sestrice, Muline and Frnaža: the full-day bays

These three are the fixed points of our day sailing route.

Tri Sestrice, the Three Sisters, are a scatter of low islets off Rivanj, about an hour out, and we anchor at the middle one. The channels between them are the closest thing this coast has to an aquarium: shallow, sheltered, sand and seagrass on the bottom, clear enough that people confidently misjudge six metres for two. A small unmanned lighthouse, built in 1899, stands on one of them. This is reliably the stop where the day's schedule starts to slip.

Muline, on the northern tip of Ugljan, is the lunch anchorage, and it has the strangest backstory of any swimming spot near Zadar. In the first century AD this bay ran an olive oil works: a Roman villa rustica with five presses and an estimated 85,000 litres a season, the largest such operation known in Croatia. The outlines of a fifth-century basilica still stand among the olives behind the shore.

Frnaža, on the northeast coast of Ugljan, gets the last long swim, around half past three: pebbles, pines, water clear to the anchor chain. By late afternoon the sun is behind the ridge and the bay drops into shade, which photographs badly and swims beautifully. All three, plus the surprise stop the skipper adds when the wind allows, are in our guide to the bays you can only reach by boat.

Galevac, Ošljak, Otrić and Kostanj: four hours' worth

The half-day sail takes two swim stops out of four candidates, chosen on the morning by the wind rather than by a brochure: Galevac, Ošljak, Otrić or Kostanj.

Galevac, known locally as Školjić, is a pine-covered islet a two-minute row off Preko on Ugljan, with a 15th-century Franciscan monastery on it; people swim across from the village harbour. Ošljak, halfway over the channel, is the smallest inhabited island in the Adriatic at 0.39 square kilometres, with around 35 residents and a hill of cypresses. Otrić and Kostanj are working anchorages rather than sights. One of the four is always in the lee, which is exactly why the list has four names and the day has two stops.

Sakarun: 800 metres of sand, and no road worth taking

Sakarun is the beach that makes the postcards, and for once the postcards are honest: roughly 800 metres of pale sand on the northwest coast of Dugi Otok, shallows that run out about 350 metres before the bottom drops, old Aleppo pine behind throwing real shade at midday. The colour comes from the sand, not from a filter.

The bank of dried brown seagrass along the waterline in early summer is not neglect: those Posidonia banquettes hold the sediment in place. Arriving by land means a ferry to Brbinj, a drive across the island and a walk down from a car park, with everyone the timetable delivered at the same minute.

Under sail it is too far for a single day from Zadar, and anyone who tells you otherwise is planning to motor hard and give you an hour ashore. It works as a morning on the three-day route, or as a speedboat day. The full story is in our guide to Sakarun.

Lake Mir, Lojena and the outer edge

South of Sakarun the island ends in Telašćica, a nature park since 1988, where the Stene cliffs drop 161 metres into the open sea. Behind those cliffs, ten minutes' walk from the anchorage, is the oddest swim in the archipelago: Lake Mir, roughly 900 metres by 300, never more than six metres deep, fed by the sea through cracks in the limestone. It runs warmer and saltier than the water on the other side of the ridge, so you float in it without effort.

Further out, the Kornati national park has exactly one sandy beach in it: Lojena, on Levrnaka, tucked under the island's high side. Everything else out there is rock, which is the point of the place.

Which one for which day

Staying in the old town without a car and wanting a swim before dinner: Kolovare. Small children and a whole day to fill: Borik. An evening swim without tourists: Diklo. Those three cover most of a normal holiday and there is nothing second-rate about them.

The day the forecast says thirty-four degrees and no wind, though, the city beaches stop being pleasant and start being a queue. That is the day to be on the water. Our full-day sail takes Tri Sestrice, Muline and Frnaža and has you back at six; the half-day does two bays in four hours.

Two practical notes. The wind decides which bay, and a skipper who changes the plan at nine in the morning is doing the job properly. And a bay with no road has no shop either, so bring what you need: the list we hand every guest starts with sunscreen, a hat, more water than you expect and shoes you can walk on pebbles in.
