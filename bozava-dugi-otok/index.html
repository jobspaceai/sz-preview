Božava: a Pine Wood, a Harbour, and a Century of Quiet Tourism

On the northeastern coast of Dugi Otok, sheltered from the open sea by the entire width of the island, Božava is a small place in a bay full of pine trees. It is the most developed tourist spot on the island, which on Dugi Otok means four hotels and a harbour, and it has one genuinely unusual claim: most of the wood that makes it what it is was planted deliberately, by a village priest.

Bosane, 1327

Božava first appears in writing in 1327, under the name Bosane. People were here a great deal earlier than that: there are remains of Illyrian hillforts on the high ground behind the village, which is the standard pattern along this coast, where the prehistoric settlements sit on defensible summits and the medieval ones move down to the water once the sea stops being mainly a source of danger.

The oldest thing standing is the church of St Nicholas in the cemetery, built in the 9th or 10th century. Inside it are fragments of stone altar screens from the early Croatian period, a Gothic wooden crucifix from the 15th century, and a lintel carved with a relief of St Nicholas and the year 1469. St Nicholas is the patron of sailors and travellers, which on an island like this is less a devotional choice than a professional one.

The wood a priest planted

The pine wood that wraps around the bay is not natural cover. It grew from a reforestation effort begun on the initiative of the parish priest, don Mirko Didović, and it is now the reason Božava looks the way it does: dense pine coming right down to the water, shade over the shoreline path, and a smell of hot resin from June onwards that you can identify with your eyes shut.

The shore promenade through it is the best walk on this half of the island. It is flat, it is shaded almost the whole way, and it links a string of small coves cut into the rock, most of which have a ladder into the water and nothing else. It takes about twenty minutes to walk and about three hours if you keep stopping to swim, which is the correct way to do it.

Tourism arrived late, then stopped

Contrary to what you will read in a few places, Božava is not an Edwardian resort. Organised tourism here began after the Second World War, from about 1951, first through a local cooperative and private rooms. The first hotel, called Božava, opened in June 1960 and was rebuilt and renamed Kadulja in 1979. Today there is a hotel complex of four buildings scattered among the pines, plus private apartments in the village.

What Božava then did not do is keep growing. There is no promenade of bars, no marina development, no apartment blocks on the hillside. The coast either side is still rock, pine and coves. Whether that was policy, planning restriction or simply the friction of being on an island with a car ferry timetable, the outcome is the same, and it is why people who liked the place twenty years ago still like it.

What is within reach

Božava sits in the middle of the island's northern highlights. Sakarun, the white sand beach with 350 metres of shallows, is a short run west, and in summer a small tourist train runs across the island to it. The Veli Rat lighthouse marks the tip beyond that. The deep inlet of Luka Solišćica, with the village of Soline at its head, cuts into the island just north and is one of the best all-weather anchorages around.

On the far side of the island, facing the open Adriatic, is the coast of sea caves, including Golubinka with its collapsed roof. That side only works in settled weather, and Božava is the sensible base from which to judge whether the day is good enough to try.

The harbour, and what lies across the channel

The harbour is small but properly equipped, with moorings that have water and electricity, which is more than most places this size manage. The bays either side are good anchorages in settled weather, and because the whole village faces northeast, into the channel rather than the open sea, it stays calm in conditions that make the western side of the island unusable.

Across the water from here is Molat, with Sestrunj and the smaller islands of Rava and Rivanj further down the channel toward Zadar. It is a quiet piece of sea. On a summer afternoon you can look across it and count more empty bays than boats.

On the water

On a speedboat day Božava is the natural lunch stop between Sakarun and the lighthouse: you can be alongside, fed and back out again inside two hours. On a sailing trip it works better as an overnight, with the pine wood to walk off dinner in and a genuinely dark sky once the village goes to bed. Our three-day route can be planned around it if the forecast points north.

There is no attraction here in the ticketed sense. No entrance fee, no queue, no viewing platform. That is precisely the attraction, and if you want the opposite, Sali in festival week is at the other end of the same island.
