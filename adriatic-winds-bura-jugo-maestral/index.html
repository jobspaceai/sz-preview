Bura, Jugo, Maestral: the Three Winds That Decide Your Day

Ask a skipper on this coast what the plan is and you will get a version of the same answer: depends on the wind. Not as an excuse, as a fact. Three winds run the Adriatic, they behave completely differently, and learning what they are called makes half of what happens on your trip stop being mysterious.

Bura: named after a god, behaves like one

The bura comes down off the mainland mountains from the northeast, cold, dry and violently gusty. The name comes from the Greek boreas, and Boreas was not a gentle character: a winged, violent god described as king of all the other winds. Whoever named this wind had met it.

Senj, up the coast, is the capital of it: the bura there is the most frequent, the longest-lasting and, by average speed, the strongest on the Croatian coast, with strong winds blowing something like 133 days a year. Locals will tell you, entirely seriously, that it makes them sleep better and eat more.

In the Velebit channel it is a different proposition. In winter storms the bura there regularly tops 60 knots and there is nowhere good to hide. Near the eastern shore it flattens the water, then crosses the channel and builds high, steep waves as it goes west toward Pag, Rab and Krk. A gust of around 248 km/h has been recorded at the Maslenica bridge, the kind of number that closes a motorway.

The mechanism is katabatic. Cold, dense air pools behind the Velebit range, spills over the ridge and accelerates downhill under its own weight, arriving at sea level in violent bursts rather than a steady stream. That is why it gusts so hard: it is not one wind, it is a series of collapses. Its season runs roughly October to April, so most summer visitors never meet it.

Why Zadar gets off lightly

Here is the part that matters if you are booking a boat trip. The Zadar channel is classified as a bora lee region: rarely hit by the bura and much less dangerous for sailing than the water an hour north of it. The mountains and the geometry of the coast do the work, and our archipelago sits in the quiet corner behind it.

The islands help too. Ugljan, Pašman, Dugi Otok, Molat and Sestrunj lie in long lines parallel to the coast, so swell from the open Adriatic breaks up on the outer islands before it reaches the water we sail in, and there is nearly always a lee shore to duck behind. None of which is marketing. It is geography, and it is why this stretch of coast is such a forgiving place to learn to sail, bring children, or take a trip when somebody aboard is nervous about boats.

Jugo: the one that brings weather

The jugo blows from the southeast, warm and humid, and unlike the bura it does not gust: it settles in and pushes. What it builds is swell and a heavy sea, usually with cloud and rain behind it. It is the wind that explains why some days we shorten a route or move a swim stop to the other side of an island.

It also has a reputation ashore for making everyone irritable, a folk belief taken seriously enough in Dalmatia that it once turned up as a mitigating circumstance in courtrooms. What is certain is that the jugo takes a day to build and a day to die, so unlike the bura it surprises nobody. We plan around it rather than react to it.

Maestral: the good wind

And then the one we actually want. The maestral is a thermal wind, driven by land and sea heating at different rates. The land warms through the morning, the air above it rises, cooler air comes in off the sea to replace it, and by early afternoon there is a steady breeze from the northwest. It arrives with settled weather, peaks in July and August, usually blows force 3 to 5, and dies away in the evening.

Sailors call it the good wind and mean it literally: reliable enough to plan a day around, strong enough to sail properly, gentle enough that nobody aboard is frightened. Almost every photograph of a boat under full sail on this coast is a photograph of the maestral doing its job.

Burin: the one that blows at night

The maestral has a night shift called the burin. Once the sun goes down the land cools faster than the sea, the circulation reverses, and a light breeze runs from the land out to the water until early morning. It is gentle, it comes with clear skies, and it is why a bay that felt airless at dinner is comfortable by midnight. It also swings every boat at anchor, which anyone sleeping aboard on our multi-day route tends to notice at about two in the morning.

Neverin: the summer ambush

The one wind here genuinely worth respecting in summer is the neverin, a short, violent squall that comes with thunderstorms. It arrives from the northwest, west or southwest, builds a nasty short sea fast, and is over within the hour. Thunder and lightning announce it, which at night is the easiest way to locate one before it reaches you.

They become more likely from August onward. They are also the reason a skipper will occasionally cut a swim stop short on a beautiful afternoon and head for shelter while the sky is still blue: that is not caution for its own sake, it is somebody reading a cloud correctly.

How a skipper actually reads the day

The morning brief is short. Is there a bura signature in the forecast. Is a jugo building, in which case the exposed sides of the outer islands are out and we work the sheltered channels. What time will the maestral fill in, because that decides which leg gets sailed rather than motored. And is anything convective forecast for late afternoon, which sets how far from shelter we are willing to be at four o'clock. Everything else, which bay and in what order, follows from those four answers. That is why the route is a plan rather than a timetable.

What this means for your trip

On our day sailing trip, the morning is usually calm motoring or light sailing, and the maestral fills in for the afternoon leg: that is when the sails go up properly and the day earns its name. On the half-day trip, whether we sail or motor depends on what the wind is doing in those four hours, which is why we promise the swimming and not the sailing. The evening sail catches the maestral on its way out: the softest sailing of the day.

If the bura is forecast, we will tell you honestly and move the date, free of charge. For what each month usually delivers, read our month-by-month guide, and take the windproof jacket whatever the forecast says. On this coast, arguing with a wind named after a Greek god has never worked out well for anyone.
