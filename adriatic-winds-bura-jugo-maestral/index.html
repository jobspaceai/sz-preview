Bura, Jugo, Maestral: the Three Winds That Decide Your Day

Ask a skipper on this coast what the plan is and you will get a version of the same answer: depends on the wind. Not as an excuse, as a fact. Three winds run the Adriatic, they behave completely differently, and each of them has been shaping life here for as long as anyone has been writing things down.

Bura: named after a god, behaves like one

The bura comes down off the mainland mountains from the northeast, cold, dry and violently gusty. The name comes from the Greek boreas, and in Greek mythology Boreas was not a gentle character: a winged, violent god described as king of all the other winds. Whoever named this wind had met it.

It is at its worst where cold air funnels through mountain passes and accelerates. Senj, up the coast, is the capital of it: the bura there is the most frequent, the longest-lasting and, by average speed, the strongest on the Croatian coast, with strong winds blowing something like 133 days a year. Locals will tell you, entirely seriously, that the bura makes them sleep better and eat more.

In the Velebit channel it is a different proposition altogether. In winter storms the bura there regularly reaches force 12, and there is nowhere good to hide. Near the eastern shore it flattens the water; then it crosses the channel and builds high, steep waves as it goes west toward Pag, Rab and Krk.

Why Zadar gets off lightly

Here is the part that matters if you are booking a boat trip. The Zadar channel is classified as a bora lee region: an area rarely hit by the bura and, correspondingly, much less dangerous for sailing than the water an hour north of it. The mountains and the geometry of the coast do the work; the wind's worst behaviour happens elsewhere, and our archipelago sits in the quiet corner behind it.

That is not marketing, it is geography, and it is a large part of why this stretch of coast is such a forgiving place to learn to sail, bring children, or spend a first day on a boat.

Jugo: the one that brings weather

The jugo blows from the southeast, warm and humid, and unlike the bura it does not gust: it settles in and pushes. What it builds is swell, high waves and a heavy sea, usually with cloud and rain behind it. It is the wind that changes plans, and the one that explains why some days we shorten a route or move a swim stop to the other side of an island.

It also has a reputation ashore for making everyone irritable, a folk belief taken seriously enough in Dalmatia that it once turned up as a mitigating circumstance in courtrooms. Make of that what you will.

Maestral: the good wind

And then the one we actually want. The maestral is a thermal wind: the land heats up through the morning, the air rises, cooler air comes in off the sea to replace it, and by early afternoon there is a steady breeze from the northwest. It arrives with good weather, peaks in July and August, and dies away in the evening.

Sailors call it the good wind, and mean it literally. It is reliable enough to plan a day around, strong enough to sail properly, and gentle enough that nobody aboard is frightened. Almost every photograph of a sailing boat under full canvas on this coast is a photograph of the maestral doing its job.

What this means for your trip

On our day sailing trip, the morning is usually calm motoring or light sailing, and the maestral fills in for the afternoon leg: that is when the sails go up properly and the day earns its name. On the half-day trip, whether we sail or motor depends entirely on what the wind is doing in those four hours, which is why we promise the swimming and not the sailing.

And if the bura is forecast, we will tell you honestly and move the date. On this coast, arguing with a wind named after a Greek god has never worked out well for anyone.
