Iž: the Only Adriatic Island Famous for Pottery

Every island in this archipelago lived off the same three things: olives, sheep and fish. Iž added a fourth, and it made the island's name. Here they made pots, and they made them well enough that people on other islands, who had perfectly good clay of their own, bought from Iž instead.

Two villages, two trades

Iž is twelve kilometres long and sits in the middle of the archipelago, between Ugljan and Dugi Otok. There are two settlements, both on the eastern shore: Veli Iž in its bay to the north, and Mali Iž, which is really three hamlets, Muće, Makovac and Porovac. Constantine VII recorded the island as Ez in the 10th century, there are traces of an Illyrian hillfort and a Roman settlement on it, and Mali Iž still has an 11th century Romanesque church of St Mary. From 1409 the island was Venetian, like everything else here.

The odd part is the division of labour. Clay is found all over the island, but only Veli Iž became a village of potters. Mali Iž fished. Two settlements a short walk apart, one making pots and the other catching sardines, for centuries, without either taking up the other's trade.

A clay economy

The first written record of Iž pottery dates to 1530, and the craft is certainly older than the document. For centuries afterwards the island's wood-fired ware travelled a market that stretched from Rab in the north to Boka Kotorska in the south, and inland to places that had never seen the sea. The boats went out loaded with pots and came back with grain, wine and whatever else the mainland had that a rocky island did not.

These were not decorative objects. The Iž potters made lopiže, kogume, čripnje and fogune: cooking pots, coffee pots, baking bells and portable stoves. Everyday equipment, thrown on a low hand-turned wheel using three tools, a scraper, an awl and a spatula, and fired in a wood kiln. The shapes were fixed by function and by generations of repetition, which is why an Iž pot is recognisable at a glance to anyone who grew up around one.

The last lopižarka

The craft nearly died in the twentieth century, as crafts do when a factory pot costs less than a handmade one. What kept it alive was a small number of families passing it down. Today the tradition is carried by Nika Petrović Grilc, a ceramicist who learned it in childhood from her father, Predrag Petrović, and took it over after his death. She is generally described, accurately and a little bleakly, as the last lopižarka. The Croatian Ministry of Culture has listed the skill as intangible cultural heritage, and she now runs workshops, including for students from Zadar, which is the only thing that ever actually saves a craft.

In Veli Iž the ethnographic collection holds local and imported ceramics alongside costumes, weaving tools, wool, linen and cotton work and shoemaking gear. It is an honest picture of what an island economy looked like before tourism, and it takes twenty minutes.

The King of Iž

On 29 July every year Veli Iž holds the Iška fešta, and the centre of it is the election of a king. This is not a tourist invention. Electing a village king or doge is a medieval European custom that was once widespread across these islands and has survived, as far as anyone can tell, only here. The elected king serves a one-year mandate over Iž, Rava and Lavdara, delivers a speech, and is expected to take the job exactly as seriously as it deserves.

Around it there are boat races, old dances, costumes out of the chest, and a great deal of food. If you are anchored anywhere within reach on the last weekend of July, go.

Korinjak, and the rest of the island

Behind the two villages the island rises to Korinjak at 168 metres, and the walk up is the standard reason to leave the harbour. From the top you get the archipelago in cross-section: Ugljan and Rivanj to the east, Rava directly south, Dugi Otok closing the western horizon, and the channels between them running north to south like lanes. Below Veli Iž there is a wooded shore with swimming spots that have no road to them, and on the seaward side a coastline of small coves that stay empty even in August because getting to them requires a boat and a reason.

The one building on the island that counts as an institution is the hotel at Korinjak, put up in 1969 and taken over in 2000 by owners who turned it into the first hotel on the Croatian coast serving an exclusively vegetarian menu. Whatever you make of that, it tells you what sort of visitor Iž has attracted for the last quarter century: people who came for the quiet and stayed because there was nothing to interrupt it.

Sailing to Iž

Iž lies in the middle of the archipelago, which makes it a natural stop on a longer day or a private charter rather than a standard half-day run. The coast is indented and gentle, the anchorages are shallow and sheltered, and Veli Iž has a small marina if you want to walk ashore for an hour. There are no crowds here in any month, which is the point.

Immediately south lies Rava, which the King of Iž nominally reigns over, and beyond it Dugi Otok. For the wider picture see our overview of the Zadar archipelago, or look at the full-day sailing route and ask us to bend it this way.

Bring back a pot if you can find one. It will be the only souvenir on your shelf with a five-hundred-year supply chain behind it.
