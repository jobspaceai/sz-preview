Veli Rat: the Lighthouse They Say Was Built With 100,000 Egg Yolks

At the northwestern tip of Dugi Otok, where the island runs out and the open Adriatic begins, stands the tallest lighthouse on this sea. It has been working since 1849, it is still staffed, and the story people tell about how it got its colour is almost certainly too good to be entirely true. Which has never stopped anyone from telling it.

The recipe

The tower and the keeper's house are a warm ochre yellow, unusual for an Adriatic lighthouse, most of which are white. According to local tradition the builders mixed something like one hundred thousand egg yolks into the plaster to help it stand up to salt, sun and bura.

Nobody has produced a nineteenth-century invoice for a hundred thousand eggs, and the number has the suspiciously round shape of something that grew in the telling. But the technique is not invented. Lime plasters and renders were routinely enriched with organic binders across the Mediterranean, egg being one of them, and the argument for it is straightforward: the proteins help the render cure harder and shed water better. Whatever went into it, the surface has now outlasted the Austro-Hungarian Empire, two world wars, several changes of state and about 175 winters of weather off the open sea. That is a decent result for breakfast.

Do the arithmetic on the logistics, though. A hundred thousand eggs, delivered to the far tip of an island with no road, in 1849, by boat, and used before they turned. That, more than the height of the tower, is why the story survives.

Why anyone put a lighthouse here

Because this is a corner, and corners are where ships are lost. Veli Rat marks the point where the outer edge of the archipelago turns: on one side the open Adriatic running up toward Istria and Venice, on the other the sheltered channels that lead down past Molat, Sestrunj and Ugljan toward Zadar. Get that turn wrong at night, in a hard bura, and you end up on a low limestone shore that gives no warning at all.

By the middle of the 19th century Austria-Hungary had serious commercial and naval traffic running out of Trieste, Rijeka and Pula, and it embarked on a systematic programme of lighting the eastern Adriatic. Veli Rat was part of that programme, and it was built to the same recipe as the empire's other big towers: cut stone, a keeper's house at the base, a cistern, a chapel, and a family living there permanently to keep the light burning.

The numbers, and where they disagree

Veli Rat is usually described as 42 metres tall and the tallest lighthouse on the Adriatic, and that is the figure on every sign and in every guidebook. Technical registers are slightly more conservative: the masonry tower measures around 36 metres, with the focal plane of the light sitting about 41 metres above the sea, and the 42 comes from measuring the whole structure from its base. The light shows two white flashes and carries roughly 22 nautical miles, which is about 40 kilometres, so on a clear night it is visible from well beyond the horizon of the island itself.

It made the Croatian post office's lighthouse stamp series in 2010, alongside Vir and Tajer. That is the closest thing a working lighthouse gets to a medal.

The chapel, the keeper and the missal

Next to the tower is a small chapel dedicated to St Nicholas, patron saint of sailors, which is not a decorative touch: a lighthouse family in 1850 was several hours from the nearest church in good weather and unreachable in bad. The chapel holds a Roman missal printed in 1869, still there, which gives some sense of how little the place has changed.

The lighthouse is still manned, which is increasingly rare, and part of the keeper's quarters has been let as holiday accommodation since around 2000. If your idea of a holiday is a stone house at the end of an island with a pine wood behind it, a working light overhead and nothing else within walking distance, it is one of the best rooms on this coast.

Čuna, Solišćica and the water around it

The lighthouse stands on the shore of Čuna bay, part of the much larger inlet of Luka Solišćica that cuts deep into the northern end of Dugi Otok. Solišćica is one of the better all-weather anchorages in the archipelago, big enough to find shelter in almost any direction, with the village of Soline at its head. There is a shallow beach beside the lighthouse itself, sand and shingle, usually with more boats than people on it.

The pine wood behind the tower is worth ten minutes of your time. It is thin, salt-bitten and full of the smell of hot resin in August, and the path through it comes out on the western shore, where the island faces the open sea and there is nothing between you and Italy.

Doing it in a day from Zadar

The lighthouse sits a short run from Sakarun, which makes the pair an obvious single outing: white sand and turquoise shallows first, then the tower, then lunch. Božava is close enough to add as a third stop if the day is calm. Our speedboat trips take all of that in one day out of Zadar; on a sailing boat it is a longer proposition and better handled as part of a multi-day route.

Whichever way you arrive, stand at the base and look up at forty-two metres of yellow render that has been arguing with the open Adriatic since 1849. Then decide for yourself about the eggs.
