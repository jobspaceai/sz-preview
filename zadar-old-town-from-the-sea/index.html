Zadar Old Town: Two Thousand Years on a Peninsula Half a Kilometre Wide

Every trip we run starts and ends within sight of the same small peninsula: about a kilometre long, half a kilometre wide, and packed with the leftovers of everyone who ever wanted to own it. Romans, Byzantines, Venetians, Habsburgs, Italy, Yugoslavia. Each of them built something, and almost nobody bothered to clear away what came before. Before you get on a boat, it is worth understanding what you are sailing away from.

The forum, and the column of shame

Zadar's Roman forum was laid out between the 1st century BC and the 3rd century AD and was, in its day, one of the largest on this side of the Adriatic: temples, colonnades and a capitolium dedicated to Jupiter, Juno and Minerva. Most of it is now an open field of carved stone in the middle of a living city, and you can walk across it for free at three in the morning if you like, which is unusual enough on its own.

One full column still stands at the edge of it. Its second career was better than its first: in the Middle Ages it served as a pillar of shame, where offenders were chained up for the town to look at. Rome built it to hold up a roof; the Middle Ages used it to hold up a reputation.

A church made out of a forum

In the 9th century someone built a church directly on top of the forum, using its stones. St Donatus is round, tall and austere, unlike anything else on this coast, and if you look at the base you can see Roman blocks and inscriptions built straight into the walls: an early, entirely practical form of recycling.

It has not held regular services for a long time. Instead its acoustics turned it into a concert hall, and on summer evenings you can hear early music coming out of a ninth-century drum built from second-century rubble. Next to it stands the cathedral of St Anastasia with its bell tower, and the climb up is the cheapest good view in the city.

The walls that made the UNESCO list

Venice fortified Zadar heavily against the Ottomans, and the result is now part of a UNESCO World Heritage listing covering Venetian defensive works across three countries. The Land Gate of 1543 is the showpiece: the grandest of the surviving gates, carved with the winged lion of St Mark and a figure of St Chrysogonus on horseback, and still the main way into the old town on foot.

Walk through it and you are following the same line every merchant, soldier and pilgrim took for five centuries. The moat outside is now a small marina; the walls above it are a promenade.

Two squares named after water

A besieged city dies of thirst long before it dies of anything else, and Zadar's answer is still visible in two squares at the southern end of the peninsula.

Five Wells Square sits directly on top of a huge cistern built in the 16th century, when the Ottoman threat made a reliable water supply a matter of survival. Five stone wellheads stand in a row above it, and the only surviving tower of the medieval fortifications, the Captain's Tower, stands at one end. Above the square lies Queen Jelena Madijevka Park, laid out in 1829 and one of the oldest public parks in this part of Europe.

Three Wells Square, a few streets away toward the harbour, is the smaller and quieter sibling: three wellheads, a handful of cafés and, in the evening, one of the pleasantest places in the old town to sit. Between the two, you have the entire water strategy of a Renaissance city laid out in stone.

Kalelarga, and where locals actually swim

The peninsula's spine is Kalelarga, officially Široka ulica, the "wide street" that has been the main artery since Roman times and is still where the whole city walks in the evening. Locals will tell you Kalelarga is older than the city, which is not quite true and completely believable.

And when the heat becomes serious, the city does not go to the old town at all. It goes to Kolovare, the town beach a ten-minute walk southeast of the walls: concrete platforms, pebbles, pine shade, bars, and the whole of Zadar in swimming shorts from June onward. It is not the Adriatic of the postcards, but it is the Adriatic of the people who live here, which is arguably more interesting.

And then the sea

Walk the length of the peninsula and it ends at the waterfront, where two modern installations do exactly the job the forum once did: bring everyone to the same place at the same hour. The Sea Organ turns waves into music under a set of marble steps, and beside it the Greeting to the Sun collects sunlight all day and spends it after dark.

The best view of all of it, though, is from thirty metres offshore: walls, campaniles, promenade and the sun dropping behind Ugljan, all in one frame, with no one standing in front of you. That is what our evening sail is for, and why it is the trip most guests book last and remember first.
